How to Plan Your Week in 20 Minutes (iPhone Method)

Updated November 9, 2026 · 10 min read

Most weekly plans fail in one of two ways. Either they never happen, because "planning the week" sounds like an hour you don't have, or they happen once, produce a list of 23 things, and are dead by Tuesday lunchtime.

The fix is a short session with a fixed order. Twenty minutes, four steps of five minutes each: look back, look ahead, choose, place. Then a two-minute check on Wednesday, and a simple rule for replanning when the week falls apart. That's the whole method.

This guide walks through each step with the exact questions to ask, shows three example weeks (a full-time worker, a parent and a student), and ends with a setup on your iPhone that makes the session easy to repeat.

Before you start: pick a time and open three things

Choose one slot and keep it. Sunday evening works for most people because the week hasn't started yet. Friday afternoon works if you like closing the week before the weekend. Monday at 8:30 works if weekends are sacred. The slot matters less than doing it in the same slot every week.

Open three things on your iPhone before the timer starts:

  • Apple Calendar in week view, showing this week and next.
  • Apple Reminders (or Todoist, if that's where your tasks live).
  • A blank note for the session itself. You'll write about ten lines.

Set a 20-minute timer. When it rings, you stop, even if the plan feels unfinished. A rough plan you actually follow beats a perfect one you never finish making.

Step 1: Look back (5 minutes)

You can't plan next week honestly without knowing what happened to this one. Scroll back through last week's calendar and your completed reminders, and answer three questions in your note:

  1. What got done? List it, briefly. This is the part people skip, and it's the part that tells you how much a real week holds.
  2. What slipped? Anything you planned and didn't do. Write one word on why: "meetings", "sick", "underestimated", "avoided".
  3. What carries over? From the slipped list, pick what still matters. Everything else gets deleted or moved to a "someday" list. Carrying over everything is how a plan turns into a backlog.

The "why" words are the useful bit. If "underestimated" shows up every week, you need more empty space (step 4). If "avoided" keeps appearing next to the same task, that task needs to be smaller, not rescheduled again.

Step 2: Look ahead (5 minutes)

Now look forward two weeks, not one. Most weekly crises are deadlines you saw coming but only looked at when they were three days away.

  • Fixed events. Meetings, appointments, school runs, classes, travel. These are the walls of your week; everything else fits around them.
  • Deadlines in the next 14 days. Anything due this week or next. If something is due next Wednesday, it probably needs work this week.
  • Energy-heavy days. The day with back-to-back meetings, the late shift, the day after a flight. Mark them. Those days get no big outcome work, only the fixed stuff and small tasks.

By the end of this step you should be able to say, roughly, "Tuesday and Thursday are packed, Wednesday morning and Friday are open." That sentence is the map for step 4.

Step 3: Choose three outcomes, no more (5 minutes)

This is the step that makes the plan work. Pick at most three outcomes for the week: the "big rocks" that would make it a good week even if nothing else happened.

An outcome is a finished state, not an activity. Compare:

Activity (vague)Outcome (finishable)
Work on the reportSend the report draft to Sam
Sort out the houseBook the plumber and clear the spare room
Study for the examFinish practice papers 1 and 2
Exercise moreThree 30-minute runs

Use this filter when choosing: "If I only finish these three, will I be glad on Friday?" If the answer is yes, stop choosing. If one of your three is a carry-over from last week, it goes first; things that slip twice tend to slip forever.

Everything else, the emails, errands and small tasks, stays in Reminders. It still gets done, in the gaps. It just doesn't get to compete with the three.

Step 4: Place each outcome on the calendar (5 minutes)

An outcome without a time slot is a wish. Open Calendar and give each outcome one or more blocks:

  1. Big rocks go into open, high-energy slots first. Use the map from step 2. The report draft goes on Wednesday morning, not on the Thursday full of meetings.
  2. Size blocks generously. If you think it takes two hours, book three. Most people underestimate how long their own tasks take, and your "underestimated" notes from step 1 will tell you by how much.
  3. Plan a buffer. Keep one afternoon, often Friday, free of plans. It catches whatever overran, and if nothing did, it's a gift.
  4. Leave about 30% of your free time empty. Not "free for small tasks", actually empty. Real weeks contain surprises, and a plan with no slack breaks at the first one.

If the three outcomes don't fit after this, the plan is telling you something. Drop one, or shrink it ("draft the first half"). Doing that now, on Sunday, is far easier than discovering it at 4pm on Friday.

For the scheduling technique itself, time blocking and the other methods are covered in our time management best practices. This session is the weekly wrapper around them.

The Wednesday check (2 minutes)

Midweek is when plans quietly drift. Put a two-minute check on Wednesday, at lunch or end of day, and ask only this:

  • Which of the three outcomes is done, on track, or behind?
  • For the one that's behind: does it still have a slot before Friday? If not, give it one now, using the buffer if you must.

That's it. No new list, no reshuffling everything. The point is to catch the slip on Wednesday, when there are still two days to fix it, rather than in Sunday's look-back.

When the week breaks

Some weeks fall apart: a sick child, an urgent project, a bad few days. Don't try to rescue the original plan. Run a five-minute mini version instead:

  1. Cross out what's now impossible. Be honest about the days that are gone.
  2. Keep one outcome. The one that matters most or has the hardest deadline. Drop or defer the other two on purpose.
  3. Find one slot for it in what's left of the week.
  4. Write the dropped outcomes into Sunday's note so they're the first thing you see in next week's look-back.

A week where you planned three, finished one and consciously dropped two is a working plan, not a failed one. The failure mode is pretending nothing changed and ending Friday with three half-done things.

Set it up on your iPhone

The method works with paper. Your phone just makes it easier to keep doing every week, because the plan stays in front of you instead of in a notebook.

Apple Calendar and Reminders

  • A recurring event called "Plan the week", 20 minutes, in your chosen slot. Add a second recurring event on Wednesday for the 2-minute check.
  • Outcome blocks as calendar events, named as outcomes ("Send report draft"), not activities ("Report").
  • A Reminders list called "This week" for the small stuff, and a "Someday" list for what you deleted from the carry-over.

Three example weeks

Full-time worker

  • Look back: finished the client deck; the budget review slipped ("meetings").
  • Look ahead: Tuesday and Thursday full of meetings; quarterly report due next Friday.
  • Three outcomes: finish the budget review (carry-over), first draft of the quarterly report, book next month's team offsite.
  • Placed: budget review Monday 9-11; report draft Wednesday 9-12; offsite booking in a 30-minute slot Monday afternoon. Friday afternoon is the buffer.

If you're juggling several projects rather than one job, pair this with our method for keeping track of multiple projects; its project list feeds straight into step 3.

Parent

  • Look back: school forms done; the dentist call slipped ("avoided").
  • Look ahead: swimming Tuesday and Thursday, a birthday party Saturday, your partner travels Wednesday to Friday.
  • Three outcomes: book dentist appointments, buy and wrap the party present, batch-cook two dinners for the solo days.
  • Placed: dentist call Monday 9:15 (first thing, before it can be avoided again); present on Monday lunch break; batch-cooking Tuesday evening. The solo days get nothing new. That's the energy-heavy stretch.

Student

  • Look back: lab write-up handed in; essay reading slipped ("underestimated").
  • Look ahead: essay due in 9 days, midterm in 12, part-time shifts Thursday and Saturday.
  • Three outcomes: finish essay reading and outline, practice papers 1 and 2, renew the library loan before the fine.
  • Placed: reading in three 90-minute blocks (Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday mornings); practice papers Friday and Sunday; library renewal as a reminder, not a block. Essay and midterm both get Ahead countdowns.

Mistakes that sink a weekly plan

  • Planning a week you wish you had. If last week held two big outcomes, this week won't hold six. Step 1 is there to keep you honest.
  • Skipping the look-back because last week was bad. Bad weeks are the most useful ones to review.
  • Filling every free hour. The 30% empty rule feels wasteful until Tuesday, when something urgent lands and your plan survives it.
  • Turning the session into an hour. If it regularly runs long, you're doing task management, not planning. Keep the timer.
  • Rescheduling the same task every week. After the second slip, shrink it, delegate it or drop it.

Questions

What is the best day to plan your week?

Whichever slot you'll protect every week. Sunday evening is the most common because the week hasn't started; Friday afternoon suits people who like to close the week before the weekend.

How long should weekly planning take?

About 20 minutes: five each for looking back, looking ahead, choosing up to three outcomes and placing them on the calendar. Add a two-minute check on Wednesday.

How many goals should I set for a week?

Three outcomes at most. Smaller tasks still get done, but they live in your reminders list and fit around the three rather than competing with them.

How do I plan my week on iPhone?

Use Apple Calendar for fixed events and outcome blocks, and Apple Reminders for small tasks. Left adds a widget that shows the week draining, countdowns for deadlines, a weekly "Plan the week" habit, and, with Left Premium, a Planner week layout that puts calendar, reminders and habits together.

What should I do when my weekly plan falls apart?

Don't rescue the whole plan. Keep the single most important outcome, give it one slot in the days left, and write the dropped outcomes into next week's look-back.
