The Best Productivity Apps for iPhone in 2027, One App per Job

Updated December 22, 2026 · 11 min read

There is no single best productivity app for iPhone, and the apps that try to be one usually do five things adequately. What works is a small setup: one app for each job, each doing that job properly, with nothing overlapping. A task app holds what you have to do. A calendar holds when. Notes hold what you know. Then a few smaller tools keep you on track: a focus timer, a habit tracker, and something that shows how much time you actually have left.

This guide is organized that way: for each job, what the built-in Apple app already covers and which dedicated apps do it better. At the end there's a simple stack that works for most people.

Disclosure: we make Left, so we've put it first. Here's why, and where another app suits you better.

One app per job

Most productivity setups fail in one of two ways. Either everything lives in one giant app you stop opening, or the same task sits in three apps and none of them is the truth. Splitting by job fixes both. Each job answers one question:

Tasks: the app that holds what you have to do

A task app has one job: be the single place every to-do goes, so your head doesn't have to hold them. Pick one and send everything there. The best one is the one you trust enough to stop keeping a second list.

Apple Reminders

Reminders is better than its reputation: lists, subtasks, tags, smart lists, shared lists and location-based reminders, synced through iCloud with no account to set up.

Best for: anyone who hasn't yet hit a wall with it. Start here.

Pros: free, built in, works with Siri, Shortcuts and plenty of other apps, including Left's Planner.

Cons: Apple devices only; weaker at projects with many stages; no real "plan my week" view.

Things 3

Things is a calm, carefully designed task manager for Apple devices. Tasks flow through Today, Upcoming, Anytime and Someday, and bigger work lives in projects grouped into areas of your life.

Best for: one person on Apple devices who wants a task app that feels pleasant to open.

Pros: excellent design; a clear daily list; paid once rather than a subscription.

Cons: Apple-only; no shared lists for working with other people; bought separately for each platform.

Todoist

Todoist is the cross-platform choice. It runs almost everywhere, understands typed dates like "every Monday at 9", and handles projects, labels, filters and shared projects well.

Best for: people who switch between iPhone and Android, Windows or a browser, or share task lists with others.

Pros: fast natural-language entry; works on nearly every platform; connects to many other tools, including Left's Planner.

Cons: some of the useful features need the paid plan; less visually calm than Things.

TickTick

TickTick combines a task manager with a calendar view, a built-in Pomodoro timer and a habit tracker, across many platforms.

Best for: people who would rather have one app cover tasks, scheduling and focus than run several.

Pros: a lot in one place; cross-platform; a free tier to try it on.

Cons: the all-in-one approach means each part is less specialized; it can become the giant app you stop opening.

Calendar and planning: when you're doing it

Tasks without times tend to sit on a list for weeks. A calendar turns "write the report" into "write the report, Tuesday 9 to 11". You don't need to schedule every task, only the ones that need a block of real time.

Apple Calendar

The built-in Calendar handles iCloud, Google and Exchange accounts side by side, shows up in widgets and Siri, and is fine for most people.

Pros: free; reads all your accounts; works everywhere on iPhone. Cons: slower to add events; no tasks next to your events.

Fantastical

Fantastical is a calendar app for people who live in their calendar. You type "Lunch with Sam Friday at 1" and it creates the event, and it can show your reminders and tasks alongside events.

Pros: fast event entry; a clearer daily and weekly view. Cons: the best features are part of a subscription; overkill if your calendar has three events a week.

Structured

Structured lays your day out as a visual timeline of time blocks, mixing tasks and calendar events, so you can see whether your plan fits in the hours you have.

Pros: makes time-blocking quick on a phone; a clear picture of the day. Cons: another place to plan if you already use a calendar heavily.

If you'd rather not move to a new calendar at all, Left's Planner reads the Calendar, Reminders and Todoist you already use and puts them next to your habits. Notes: where you put what you know

Notes are for ideas, meeting notes, reference and anything that isn't a task. Keep tasks out of your notes app, or they'll be forgotten there.

  • Apple Notes: fast, free and built in, with folders, tags, checklists, document scanning and Quick Note. For most people it's all they need.
  • Notion: a workspace of pages and databases. Powerful for projects, wikis and team docs, but best set up on a bigger screen; on iPhone it's better for reading and quick edits than for building.
  • Obsidian: notes stored as plain Markdown files that you link to each other. Ideal if you want your notes to last decades and grow into a web of ideas, less ideal if you just want a shopping list.

Rule of thumb: if you can't say what Apple Notes is failing to do for you, stay with Apple Notes.

Focus: starting and staying on one thing

A focus timer turns "work on the report" into "work on the report for 25 minutes", which is much easier to start. Two different tools get confused here, so it's worth separating them:

  • Apple's Focus modes (Settings, then Focus) silence notifications and can switch to a different Home Screen page while you work. They are not a timer.
  • A focus timer counts down a work session and a break. The Clock app's timer works in a pinch. Left's Focus is a Pomodoro-style timer with adjustable session and break lengths, a Live Activity, a widget with Start and Pause buttons, and a history of your sessions. Focus is part of Left Premium. Forest takes a different approach: it grows a virtual tree while you stay off your phone, which suits people whose main distraction is the phone itself.

The two work well together: a Work Focus mode to quiet the phone, and a timer to give the session a shape. More on the timer side on the Pomodoro timer for iPhone page.

Habits: the small things that keep the system running

A productivity setup lives or dies on a few routines: a weekly review, planning tomorrow before you stop, clearing the inbox. Those are habits, and a task app handles them badly because recurring tasks pile up as overdue and carry no sense of progress.

  • Left: flexible schedules, up to 4 reminders per habit, and habit strength, which doesn't drop to zero after one missed day. Try "Plan tomorrow, weekdays at 5 pm" and "Weekly review, once a week".
  • Streaks: a well-known, paid-once habit app with a short daily checklist and Apple Health auto-completion.
  • Productive: groups habits into morning, afternoon and evening routines.

Keep the list to three or four habits that support your work. Ten habits is a second job.

Time awareness: seeing how much time is left

This is the job most setups skip. You can have a perfect task list and still lose an afternoon because nothing told you it was passing. A day grid on your Home Screen, like Left's Time Left widget, shows how much of today is gone; a countdown widget keeps your next deadline in view for weeks, not just the day before. Put one on your Lock Screen and you'll see it dozens of times a day, which is the point.

The Apple built-ins that tie it together

  • Reminders and Calendar are the hubs many other apps read from, including Fantastical and Left's Planner. Even if you use another task app, keeping a few things in Reminders costs nothing.
  • Focus modes give you a quiet work phone and a separate personal one, each with its own Home Screen and Lock Screen.
  • Shortcuts glues apps together. One shortcut can turn on Work Focus, start a timer and open your task list. Many apps, Left included, add their own actions, so you can log a habit with Siri without opening the app.

A simple stack that works

If you want one setup to start with and adjust later, use this:

  1. Tasks: Apple Reminders. Move to Things 3 if you want more structure on Apple devices, or Todoist if you need other platforms.
  2. Calendar: Apple Calendar. Block time for the two or three tasks each week that need it.
  3. Notes: Apple Notes.
  4. Time, habits and focus: Left. Add the Time Left day grid and a countdown to your next deadline as widgets, set up two habits ("Plan tomorrow" and "Weekly review"), and use Planner and Focus if you go Premium.
  5. Glue: a Work Focus mode with its own Home Screen page holding your task list, calendar and Left widgets.

That's three free built-ins and one app. Most people never need more. If you work on a Mac as well, the best productivity apps for Mac lists the desktop side, and if ADHD shapes how you work, the best productivity apps for ADHD builds a stack around that.

How to choose

  • Start with the built-in app for each job. Replace it only when you can name what it's failing to do.
  • One app per job. If two apps both hold tasks, one of them will be wrong. Pick one and delete the other.
  • Check your platforms. Apple-only apps like Things are lovely until you need a Windows laptop at work. Cross-platform apps like Todoist cost a little polish and save a migration later.
  • Fill the gaps before you swap. Most setups don't need a better task app; they need the missing pieces: a focus timer, two habits, and something that shows the time going by.

Questions

What is the best productivity app for iPhone?

There isn't one app that does everything well. Most people do best with one app per job: a task app such as Reminders, Things 3 or Todoist, a calendar, a notes app, and Left for time awareness, habits and focus.

Are Apple's built-in apps good enough for productivity?

For many people, yes. Reminders, Calendar and Notes cover tasks, scheduling and notes well. What they don't cover is habits, focus timing and seeing how much time is left, which is where a dedicated app helps.

Is Left a to-do list app?

No. Left is a time awareness app with habits, countdowns, a Pomodoro-style Focus timer and a Planner. Planner shows your Apple Reminders and Todoist tasks next to your calendar and habits, but you keep using Reminders or Todoist for the tasks themselves.

What's the difference between Focus modes and a focus timer?

Focus modes are an iOS feature that silences notifications and can switch Home Screen pages. A focus timer, like Left's Focus, counts down work sessions and breaks. Many people use both at once.
