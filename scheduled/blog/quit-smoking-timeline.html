Quit Smoking Timeline: What Changes After You Stop, and How to Track Your Smoke-Free Days

Updated December 29, 2026 · 6 min read

The day you stop smoking, the benefits start, and they keep adding up for years. Knowing what happens when helps in two ways: it gives you milestones to look forward to, and it warns you which days are likely to be hardest, so you can plan for them instead of being surprised.

Below is a general quit smoking timeline based on what major health bodies such as the CDC, the NHS and the WHO describe, a plan for the first two weeks, and how to track your smoke-free days on iPhone. Everyone's body is different, and this isn't medical advice. Your doctor or pharmacist can help with nicotine replacement or medication, which make quitting more likely to stick.

The quit smoking timeline

These are the changes most often described by health organizations. Timings are approximate and vary from person to person.

Time smoke-freeWhat generally happens
20 minutesHeart rate and blood pressure start to come down.
8 to 12 hoursCarbon monoxide in your blood drops, and oxygen levels improve.
2 to 3 daysNicotine is mostly out of your body. Taste and smell often start to improve. Cravings and irritability tend to peak around now.
2 to 12 weeksCirculation improves, and walking or exercise often feels easier.
1 to 9 monthsCoughing and shortness of breath decrease as the lungs clear.
1 yearThe extra risk of coronary heart disease is about half that of someone who still smokes.
5 to 10 yearsRisks of several cancers, including mouth, throat and bladder, drop substantially, and stroke risk keeps falling.
10 yearsThe risk of dying from lung cancer is about half that of someone who still smokes.
15 yearsThe risk of coronary heart disease is close to that of someone who never smoked.

The early milestones come fast, which is why counting matters most at the start. Day 3 is a real achievement, not a small one.

The hardest days, and what to plan for them

Withdrawal is temporary, and it's predictable enough to plan around:

  • Days 1 to 3: cravings, irritability and trouble concentrating usually peak. Plan easy days if you can. Keep your hands and mouth busy: water, gum, a walk.
  • Week 1 to 2: cravings become less constant but still strong around triggers: coffee, a drink, the end of a meal, a stressful call. Change the routine around each trigger rather than relying on willpower.
  • Weeks 2 to 4: most physical symptoms ease. The risk now is the "just one" thought. One usually leads to more.
  • Months 2 and beyond: occasional strong cravings still show up, often linked to places or people. They pass, usually within minutes.

A craving typically fades within a few minutes if you don't act on it. Having a plan for those minutes, like a walk, a glass of water or messaging someone, is more useful than trying to tough it out.

Why counting smoke-free days helps

A visible count turns an absence into something you can see growing. "I didn't smoke today" is easy to forget by evening; "Day 19" on your Lock Screen is not. It also gives you something to protect. People who track a streak often describe the number itself as a reason to get through a hard craving.

The counter you choose matters less than seeing it often. Put it where you'll look many times a day.

A first-two-weeks plan

  1. Pick a quit date in the next two weeks. Close enough to keep momentum, far enough to prepare. Add it as a countdown.
  2. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist. Nicotine replacement and prescription options are widely used and can make quitting more likely to stick.
  3. List your triggers. Morning coffee, the commute, breaks at work, drinks. Write one swap for each.
  4. Clear it out. Cigarettes, lighters and ashtrays, the day before.
  5. Tell people. Friends and family can help, and in many countries free quitline and text-message programs exist (in the US, 1-800-QUIT-NOW).
  6. Day 1: start the streak. Put it on your Lock Screen.
  7. Days 1 to 14: one day at a time. Each evening, look at the number. That's the whole job.

Questions

What happens to your body after you quit smoking?

Health bodies describe heart rate and blood pressure starting to fall within about 20 minutes, carbon monoxide levels dropping within a day, circulation and breathing improving over weeks and months, and heart disease and cancer risks falling steadily over the years. Timings vary from person to person.

What is the hardest day when you quit smoking?

For many people, the first three days are the hardest, when nicotine leaves the body and cravings and irritability peak. Cravings usually become less frequent after the first couple of weeks.

How long do nicotine cravings last?

A single craving usually passes within a few minutes. Strong cravings are most frequent in the first weeks and become occasional over the following months.

How do I track how long since I quit smoking on iPhone?

Use a day counter or streak app. In Left, add a streak in Since with your quit date and add a streak widget to your Lock Screen. Our sobriety calculator also counts the days since any date.

Should I quit smoking cold turkey or cut down?

Both can work. Many people find it easier with nicotine replacement or medication, so it's worth talking to a doctor or pharmacist about which approach suits you.
