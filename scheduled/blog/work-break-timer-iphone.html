Work Break Timer on iPhone: Remember to Stop (and Start Again)

Updated January 20, 2027 · 9 min read

Most people don't need a reminder to work. The inbox and the deadline do that job. What slips is the other half: stopping before your eyes and back give out, then coming back instead of drifting into your phone for half an hour.

So a good work break timer does two things. It tells you when to stop, and it tells you when to start again. This guide shows how to pick a work-break rhythm that suits your job, then four ways to set one up on iPhone, from the free Clock app to a focus timer that runs the whole cycle for you.

Why breaks matter at a desk

Three things wear down during a long screen session, and a short break helps each one:

  • Your eyes. Staring at a screen at one distance tires them, and you blink less while you concentrate. A common guideline is the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at something about 20 feet (6 meters) away for 20 seconds. The American Academy of Ophthalmology gives similar advice: look up from the screen and at something in the distance as a habit.
  • Your body. Sitting still in one posture is what makes necks, shoulders and lower backs complain. A common rule of thumb is to stand up and move every 30 to 60 minutes, even for a minute or two.
  • Your attention. Focus fades over a long stretch, and you often don't notice until you've read the same paragraph three times. A planned break resets it before it runs dry, so the next block starts sharp.

The catch: the moment you most need a break is the moment you least notice the time. A timer makes the decision for you, ahead of time.

Choose a rhythm first

Before you pick a tool, pick a rhythm. The right one depends on the work, not on what's popular.

RhythmWork / breakSuits
Pomodoro25 min / 5 min, longer break after 4Starting tasks you're avoiding, admin, email triage, studying, anyone who finds long blocks daunting
50/1050 min / 10 minWriting, design, analysis: work that needs warm-up time but not a whole morning
90/1590 min / 15 minDeep work like coding or long drafts, for people who already focus easily once started

A simple decision rule: if getting started is your problem, use 25/5. If being interrupted mid-thought is your problem, use 50/10. Only move to 90/15 once 50/10 feels too short most days. Whichever you choose, keep the 20-20-20 habit inside longer blocks, because your eyes don't wait 90 minutes.

Four ways to set a work break timer on iPhone

1. The Clock app timer

The simplest option, already on your phone and free.

  1. Open Clock and tap Timers.
  2. Set your work length, for example 50 minutes.
  3. Tap When Timer Ends and pick a sound you'll hear but won't hate.
  4. Tap Start. When it rings, set a second timer for the break.

Good for: one-off breaks, or if you only want a single nudge to stop.

The limit: it has no cycle. Every phase is a new timer you have to set by hand, and the break timer is the one people skip.

2. Apple Watch movement and breathing reminders

If you wear an Apple Watch, it already nudges you to move. In the Watch app on your iPhone, under Activity, turn on Stand Reminders: the watch taps your wrist when you've been sitting for most of an hour. The Mindfulness app can also remind you to take a minute to breathe at times you set.

Good for: people whose main problem is sitting still, with no setup beyond a switch.

The limit: these reminders run on the watch's schedule, not your work blocks. A stand nudge can land in the middle of a hard thought, and nothing tells you when to come back.

3. A Shortcuts automation or Focus schedule for lunch

The longest break of the day is the one most often eaten by "just one more thing". Automate it.

With Shortcuts:

  1. Open Shortcuts, tap Automation, then the + button.
  2. Choose Time of Day, set your lunch time (say 12:30) and pick weekdays.
  3. Choose Run Immediately.
  4. Add a Show Notification action ("Lunch. Away from the desk.") or a Start Timer action set to your lunch length, so it also tells you when to come back.

With a Focus schedule: in Settings > Focus, give your Work Focus a schedule that ends at lunch, or create a Lunch Focus that turns on at 12:30. Seeing the Focus change on your Lock Screen is a clear, quiet signal that the morning is over.

Good for: one fixed break a day that should happen whatever you're doing.

The limit: it's clock-based. It doesn't know when you started working, so it can't run short breaks around your actual work blocks.

4. A focus timer that runs the work-break cycle

If you want both reminders, stop and start again, without setting timers all day, use a dedicated focus timer. Left's Focus timer (part of Left Premium) is built for this. It runs three phases: Focus, short break and long break. The defaults are 25 / 5 / 20 minutes with a long break after 4 sessions, and you can set each phase anywhere from 1 to 60 minutes, so 50/10 is two quick changes.

What makes it work as a break timer:

  • A notification at the end of each phase. When a session ends, you get "Focus session complete. Time for a break." When the break ends, you get told it's time to focus again. Left doesn't play its own sounds; the notification uses your iPhone's normal alert.
  • Continue automatically. Turn it on and Left moves into the next phase without waiting for you to tap Start. The switch shows when the app or widget next refreshes, so treat the notification as your signal and the timer as the record.
  • A Live Activity on the Lock Screen and in the Dynamic Island, so you can see how long is left in the break without opening anything.
  • A Focus widget for the Home Screen and Lock Screen with Start and Pause buttons. Coming back from a break is one tap, right where you'd otherwise start scrolling. (For placing widgets, see how to put a timer on your iPhone Home Screen.)
  • A day strip of small pills under the timer showing today's focus and break sessions. If the strip is all focus and no break, you can see it.

Good for: a full day of work blocks where you want to be told both when to stop and when to start.

The limit: it's a timer, not a blocker. It won't stop you opening another app during a break; it tells you clearly that the break is over.

Get the focus timer

Which one to use

  • You just want one nudge today: the Clock app.
  • You sit too long and already wear a watch: Stand Reminders, plus one of the others for your work blocks.
  • You keep working through lunch: a Shortcuts automation or Focus schedule.
  • You want a rhythm all day, breaks included: a focus timer like Left's.

They combine well: a focus timer for your blocks plus a lunch automation covers most desk days.

"Keep going when time ends": on or off?

Some people hate being interrupted. Twenty minutes into finally understanding a bug, a break alert feels like sabotage. Left has an option for that: Keep going when time ends. When it's on, the timer counts the overtime past zero instead of ending the phase, until you choose to move on. (It works instead of Continue automatically, not alongside it.)

Turn it on if: your work has natural stopping points you can feel, like finishing a function or a section, and you trust yourself to take the break when you reach one. The overtime count keeps you honest.

Leave it off if: "five more minutes" usually turns into an hour, your eyes or back are already complaining, or the whole reason you wanted a break timer is that you don't stop on your own. In that case, the interruption is the feature.

What to do in a 5-minute break

Pick from a fixed list instead of deciding each time:

  • Stand up and walk to another room, or outside if you can.
  • Look out of a window at the farthest thing you can see.
  • Refill your water or make a drink.
  • Roll your shoulders, stretch your neck, touch your toes.
  • Do one small physical chore: empty the dishwasher, water a plant.
  • Close your eyes and breathe slowly for a minute.

What not to do

  • Check email or chat. It's still work, and it hands your attention to someone else's agenda.
  • Scroll social media or news. Your eyes stay at the same distance, your posture doesn't change, and five minutes becomes twenty.
  • Start "a quick task". If it can't be done standing up and away from the screen, it isn't a break.

Coming back: the half people forget

A break that never ends is just a longer distraction. Three habits make the return easy:

  1. Leave a breadcrumb. Before the break, write the very next step on a sticky note or the top of your document: "Reply to Sam's second question." Coming back to a clear next step takes seconds; coming back to "where was I?" takes ten minutes.
  2. Make starting one tap. Put the timer where you'll see it when you pick up your phone: a widget on the first Home Screen page or the Lock Screen. If you use Left, the widget's Start button sits right there.
  3. Treat the end-of-break alert like a meeting. You wouldn't leave a colleague waiting. Don't leave your own work block waiting either.

Questions

How often should I take a break from computer work?

A common rule of thumb is a short break every 25 to 60 minutes, depending on the work, plus a longer one every couple of hours. For your eyes, the 20-20-20 guideline suggests looking about 20 feet away for 20 seconds every 20 minutes.

Can I set a repeating work and break timer on iPhone?

The Clock app's timer doesn't repeat a work-break cycle, so you'd reset it each time. A focus timer app such as Left's Focus timer alternates focus, short break and long break phases for you, with a notification at the end of each one.

Is the 25/5 Pomodoro rhythm the best one?

Not for everyone. 25/5 is great for starting tasks you're avoiding, while 50/10 or 90/15 suit work that needs longer warm-up time. Try one for a week, then adjust.
