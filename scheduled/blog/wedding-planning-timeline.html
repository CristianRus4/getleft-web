Wedding Planning Timeline: What to Do 12 Months Out to the Day After

Updated December 26, 2026 · 10 min read

Most wedding checklists tell you what to do in month nine but not why. That works until your engagement is shorter than the checklist, your venue has a cancellation, or your partner asks why the invitations can't wait another month. Without the reason, you can't move anything safely.

This timeline gives every task a reason. Almost all of them come down to four lead times: how far ahead people book, how long guests need to reply, how long clothes take to make and fit, and how long the legal side takes where you live. Once you see those, you can stretch or squeeze the plan to fit your date. Below: the full timeline, a 6-month version, a way to split the work between you, and a shared countdown on iPhone.

The four lead times behind every wedding timeline

  • Booking lead time. Venues, photographers, bands and celebrants can only do one wedding a day, and the popular ones on popular dates go first. Anything that can be booked by someone else goes near the top.
  • Reply time. Guests need weeks to answer, and your caterer and venue need final numbers before the day. Invitations are timed backward from those two deadlines.
  • Making and fitting time. Made-to-order outfits can take months to arrive, and alterations usually need two or three fittings spread over several weeks. That's why outfit shopping starts so early.
  • Paperwork time. What makes a marriage legal varies a lot by country, and sometimes by region or by type of ceremony. Some places need notice in advance, specific documents, or an appointment. Look up the official government rules for where you're marrying early, and plan from what they say rather than from any general checklist, this one included.

A useful decision rule follows from this: do first whatever limits the other decisions. The date, the budget, the guest count and the venue shape everything else, so nothing decorative belongs on the list until those four are settled.

If you're starting later than the first row, don't panic and don't skip ahead. Do the rows in order, faster. The order matters more than the month labels.

The timeline, month by month

12 months or more: the three numbers and the venue

Before you visit a single venue, agree three numbers: a rough guest count (a range is fine), a total budget, and a window of possible dates.

Split the budget by percentage, not by line item. Venue and catering usually take the biggest share, often close to half of the total. Decide which two or three things matter most to you both and give them a bigger slice. Keep 5 to 10% unallocated as a buffer, because something always costs more than the quote.

Then book the venue, and the ceremony location if it's separate. Why now: the venue fixes your date and your maximum guest count, and every supplier you book next will ask for both. Once the date is set, put it somewhere you'll see it every day. We cover how below.

10 to 11 months: the one-wedding-a-day suppliers

Book the people who can only be at one wedding on your date: photographer, videographer, celebrant or officiant, band or DJ, and a planner or day coordinator if you want one. Book a caterer now too if the venue doesn't provide one. Why now: after venues, these are the scarcest thing on any given Saturday.

8 to 9 months: outfits, the wedding party, save-the-dates

  • Outfits. Start shopping and order anything made to order. Why now: production can take months, and you need time left over for fittings.
  • Wedding party. Ask them now, so they can plan their own outfits, travel and time off.
  • Save-the-dates. Send them around 6 to 8 months out, earlier for a destination wedding or guests travelling far.
  • Guest rooms. Hold a block of rooms near the venue if guests will stay over.

6 to 7 months: second-tier suppliers and the legal check

Book the florist, cake, hair and makeup, rentals and transport. These suppliers can often do more than one event a day, so they book up later. This is also the latest sensible point to read the official marriage rules for your country or region, list every document you'll need, and note any notice period or appointment in your calendar. If either of you needs a passport or document renewed, start that now. If you're marrying abroad, do all of this as early as you can.

4 to 5 months: invitations, rings and the ceremony

Order invitations so they're printed and addressed in time. Printers often quote in working days, so count back from your send date with the business days calculator rather than guessing. Order rings (engraving and resizing take time), book a menu tasting, and start planning the ceremony itself: readings, vows, music, who walks in when.

2 to 3 months: send invitations

Send invitations about 8 to 12 weeks before the day, with an RSVP deadline about 4 to 6 weeks before. Why: work backward from your caterer's final-numbers deadline (it's in your contract), leave a week or two to chase people who don't reply, and that's your RSVP date. Book your first outfit fitting and draft an order of the day.

1 month: numbers, seats and fittings

Chase missing RSVPs the day after your deadline, not a week later. Once you have numbers, do the seating plan and send final numbers to the venue and caterer by the date in their contracts. Second fitting. Confirm every supplier's arrival time and contact on the day. Check any legal step that has a deadline before the wedding is done.

2 weeks to the day

Final fitting and pickup. Schedule the last supplier payments. Send a one-page run sheet (times, addresses, phone numbers) to suppliers and the wedding party. Name one person who isn't either of you as the day-of contact, so suppliers call them, not you. On the day itself, your only job is to be there.

The day after, and the weeks that follow

Return anything hired, get outfits cleaned, and pay any remaining invoices. Write thank-you notes while you still remember who gave what. If either of you is changing your name, look up the official process where you live. Review the suppliers who were good.

The 6-month version

Six months is enough. Keep the same order and make fewer, faster decisions:

  • Weeks 1 to 2: the three numbers, then the venue. Be flexible on the date: a Friday, a Sunday or a quieter month opens far more options. A venue with in-house catering turns two bookings into one. Read the legal rules this week too, because a notice period could limit your earliest possible date.
  • Weeks 3 to 4: photographer, celebrant and music. Ask suppliers you like who else is free on your date.
  • Month 2: outfits. Buy off the rack or from a sample sale, or get a confirmed delivery date in writing before you order made to order. Skip save-the-dates and send invitations instead, or send a quick digital save-the-date this month.
  • Month 3: florist, cake, hair and makeup, rings. Invitations out by the end of the month, digital if that's faster.
  • Month 4: ceremony plan, tasting, first fitting.
  • Months 5 and 6: the same as the standard final two months: RSVP deadline, seating, final numbers, fittings, run sheet.

What to cut: DIY projects, extra suppliers you'd only half use, and decisions you can bundle. A venue package with chairs, tables and linen saves you three separate conversations.

Who owns what: splitting the work between you

Things slip when both of you assume the other one is handling it. Give each area one owner.

  • Split by area. For example, one of you owns the venue, catering and guest list; the other owns photography, music and the ceremony. Outfits are owned by whoever's wearing them. The owner researches and books; the other person gets a veto on big decisions, not a vote on every detail.
  • Give the wedding party real jobs. Chasing RSVPs from their side of the family, collecting hire items, being the day-of contact. Specific jobs get done; "let us know if you need anything" doesn't.
  • Hold a short weekly check-in. Twenty minutes, same time each week: what got booked, what's due next, what needs a joint decision.
  • Keep one shared view. One shared note or spreadsheet for details (suppliers, quotes, guest list) and one shared countdown for the dates. If the dates only live in one person's calendar, only one person feels them.

Questions

How far in advance should you start planning a wedding?

About 12 months is comfortable for a popular venue on a weekend date, and more if you want a specific venue in peak season or are marrying abroad. Six months works if you're flexible on the date and keep the supplier list short.

When should wedding invitations go out?

About 8 to 12 weeks before the wedding, with an RSVP deadline about 4 to 6 weeks before. Work backward from the date your caterer needs final numbers, and leave time to chase late replies.

What should you book first for a wedding?

The venue, once you've agreed a budget, a rough guest count and a date window. It fixes your date and capacity, which every other supplier needs to know.

When do you need to sort the legal paperwork?

It depends on where you're marrying, because notice periods, documents and appointments vary by country and region. Read the official government rules at least 6 months ahead, and earlier if you're marrying abroad.
