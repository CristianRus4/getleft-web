How to Use Time Blocking: A 5-Step Setup and iPhone Guide (2026)

Updated November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

Time blocking means giving each piece of work its own slot on the calendar, so your day is planned in hours instead of as a list you hope to get through. This guide shows how to use time blocking in practice: a five-step weekly setup, one realistic day laid out as a table, the mistakes that make people quit, and how to run the whole thing on an iPhone.

For how it compares with Pomodoro, the Eisenhower matrix and other methods, see our time management practices that work.

What time blocking is (and isn't)

A to-do list says what to do. A blocked calendar also says when, and for how long. That's the useful part: try to fit eight hours of tasks into five free hours and the plan shows you before the day does.

It isn't scheduling every minute. A good blocked day has gaps on purpose, a few long blocks for hard work, and short blocks that gather small tasks together.

How to use time blocking in 5 steps

Do this once a week, on Friday afternoon or Sunday evening. Expect 20 to 30 minutes the first time.

1. List everything the week needs

Pull tasks from wherever they live: Reminders, Todoist, email, notes, the project tracker. Include what never makes a to-do list but eats real time, like meeting prep and replying to messages.

2. Estimate each task, then pad it

Write a time next to every item, in 15-minute steps. Then add to it. People reliably underestimate how long work takes (psychologists call this the planning fallacy), so a simple rule helps: add 50% to anything you haven't done before, and 25% to familiar work. A report you think takes two hours gets three.

Now compare the total with the time you actually have. A working hours calculator gives you the raw number for the week; subtract meetings and commitments and what's left is your real budget. If the tasks don't fit, cut or move them now, not on Thursday.

3. Place the non-negotiables first

Put the fixed things on the calendar before any task: meetings, classes, school pickup, commute, lunch, workouts, sleep. These are the walls. If meetings are taking over, total them with a meeting time calculator; seeing "11 hours of meetings this week" makes it easier to decline one.

4. Block deep work at your best hours

Next, place the work that needs real thought in the hours when your head is clearest. For most people that's the morning, for some it's late afternoon. Make these blocks 60 to 120 minutes long, aim for one or two a day, and name the block after the task: "Draft sections 2 and 3 of the proposal", not "Work". Many people find three to four hours of real focus a day is their ceiling, so don't plan six.

Fill lower-energy hours with batched shallow work: email, messages, admin.

5. Add buffers and a shutdown block

Leave about 15 minutes after each long block, and one 30- to 60-minute flex block in the afternoon for whatever ran over. As a rough guide, schedule no more than 70 to 80% of your working day.

End each day with a 15-minute shutdown block: check what got done, move unfinished tasks to a new slot, and glance at tomorrow.

Why time blocking fails, and the fix for each

  • Blocks are too small. A 30-minute block for hard work mostly goes to getting started. Fix: give deep work at least 60 minutes, and gather small tasks into one 30- to 45-minute batch instead of scattering them.
  • There are no buffers. One meeting runs 20 minutes over and every block after it is wrong. Fix: 15 minutes after each long block, plus one flex block a day.
  • Estimates ignore reality. You plan from how long things should take. Fix: for one week, note the real time next to each block. Most people find a steady gap, and that gap becomes the padding in step 2.
  • The plan breaks and you drop it. Fix: take five minutes to re-block the rest of the day. A plan redrawn at 1 p.m. still beats no plan.

Variations worth knowing

  • Task batching: group similar small tasks (calls, email, errands) into one block, so you switch context once instead of ten times.
  • Day theming: give each day a theme, such as Monday for planning and admin or Wednesday for writing, and block inside it.
  • Timeboxing: set a fixed limit for a task and stop when it's up, finished or not. Time blocking reserves time; timeboxing caps it.

How to time block on iPhone

Apple Calendar is enough to start. A few extras make the blocks easier to follow.

Apple Calendar: the blocks themselves

  1. In Calendar, tap Calendars at the bottom, then Add Calendar, and make one called "Blocks" with its own color. Your blocks then stand apart from meetings, and you can hide them from the same Calendars list.
  2. In Day view, touch and hold an empty time to create an event. Name it after the task, and drag its edges to set the length.
  3. Use Repeat for the blocks that recur, like the shutdown block every weekday, and an Alert 5 minutes before so the switch doesn't take you by surprise.
  4. Optionally, set your Work Focus mode on a schedule in Settings → Focus so notifications stay quiet during your morning deep-work block.

Questions

How do I start time blocking?

Start with one week. List every task, estimate each one and add some padding, put meetings and other fixed commitments on your calendar first, then give your hardest work one or two long blocks in your best hours. Finish with buffers and a 15-minute shutdown block at the end of each day.

How long should a time block be?

For focused work, 60 to 120 minutes works for most people: long enough to get past the warm-up, short enough to stay sharp. Small tasks like email or admin work better batched into one 30- to 45-minute block than scattered across the day.

What is the difference between time blocking and timeboxing?

Time blocking reserves a slot on your calendar for a task. Timeboxing sets a hard limit and you stop when it runs out, finished or not. Many people use both: a block for the work, with a timebox so it can't spill into the rest of the day.

What is the best way to time block on iPhone?

Apple Calendar is enough to start: make a separate calendar for your blocks with its own color, create events in Day view and use Repeat for recurring blocks. To see blocks next to your reminders and habits, Left's Planner shows them in one day plan (see the Planner guide), and a Pomodoro-style timer helps you work through long blocks.

What if my day doesn't go to plan?

It won't, often, and that's fine. When something runs over, take five minutes to re-block the rest of the day: move what slipped into your flex block or to tomorrow. A plan you redraw at lunchtime still does its job.

Does time blocking work with ADHD?

Many people with ADHD find shorter, clearly named blocks and visible time cues easier to follow than a long to-do list, and generous buffers matter even more. Our guide to ADHD time blindness covers the visual side. Everyone is different, so treat it as an experiment, and talk to a clinician about anything beyond planning habits.
