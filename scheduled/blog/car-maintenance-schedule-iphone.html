Car Maintenance Schedule: What's Due When, and How to Remember

Updated January 13, 2027 · 9 min read

Most car maintenance isn't hard. An oil change, a tire pressure check, new wiper blades: none of it takes skill. What goes wrong is timing. The registration lapses by a week. The tires sit 6 psi low for a month. The service was "due soon" for half a year because the car only drives to the shops and back.

This guide does two things. First, a general schedule: what needs doing, roughly how often, and why every job runs on two clocks at once. Second, a setup on your iPhone that remembers the parts your car won't remind you about, with Left as the worked example and honest notes on where a different tool does the job better.

Every job runs on two clocks

Almost every item in a car maintenance schedule is written as "every X miles or Y months, whichever comes first". That second half matters more than people think.

  • The distance clock measures wear from driving: engine oil breaking down, tire tread wearing, brake pads thinning.
  • The time clock measures ageing that happens even when the car is parked: oil and coolant degrade, brake fluid absorbs moisture, rubber wipers and tires harden, batteries lose capacity.

Then there's a third kind of date that has nothing to do with the car's condition: paperwork. Registration, the roadworthiness inspection (an MOT in the UK, a WOF in New Zealand, a state inspection in parts of the US) and insurance renewal all fall on fixed calendar dates.

A simple decision rule: if you drive less than average, the time clock usually wins. Someone doing 4,000 miles a year will almost never reach the distance interval for an oil change, so their real reminder has to be a date. Someone doing 25,000 miles a year hits the distance limit first, and the car's service indicator or odometer is the better guide.

A general car maintenance schedule

The intervals below are common ranges, not rules for your car. Intervals vary by make, model, fuel type, age and how you drive. Towing, short trips, dusty roads and extreme heat or cold all count as "severe" use in many manuals and shorten the gaps. Your owner's manual or service book is the authority; where it disagrees with this table, follow the manual.

Bigger, rarer jobs such as spark plugs, a timing belt (if your engine has one) or transmission fluid sit in the manual at specific distances. They come up rarely enough that your service center will usually flag them, but it's worth reading those lines once so the cost doesn't surprise you.

Electric cars and hybrids

Electric cars skip oil changes, spark plugs and timing belts. They still need tires, brakes, brake fluid, wipers, cabin filters, coolant for the battery system on many models, a 12-volt battery, and the same registration, inspection and insurance dates. Tires often wear faster on an EV because of the weight and instant torque, so the monthly tire check matters more, not less. Hybrids keep most of the gas car schedule. Again, the manual decides.

The 5-minute monthly walk-around

Most of the cheap problems show up here first. Do it on the same day each month, at home, before you drive.

  1. Tires. Check pressures against the sticker in the door frame or the fuel flap, not the number on the tire. Look for nails, bulges and uneven wear.
  2. Lights. Headlights, high beams, indicators, brake lights, reverse lights. A shop window or garage door works as a mirror; otherwise ask someone to stand behind the car.
  3. Fluids. Engine oil on the dipstick (if your car has one), coolant between the marks in the reservoir, washer fluid topped up. Never open a hot coolant cap.
  4. Wipers. Run them with washer fluid. Streaks or juddering mean new blades.
  5. Underneath and inside. Fresh drips where you park, and any warning lights that stay on after starting.

Write down anything odd with the date and the odometer reading. That one line is often what a mechanic needs.

The real problem is remembering

Modern cars help, but only with part of the list. A service indicator counts down to the next oil service. A tire pressure warning lights up when a tire is well below where it should be, which is later than you'd want. Nothing in the car tells you that the registration expires next month, that the insurance renews on the 14th, or that the wipers are a year old.

The other half of the problem is records. Receipts end up in the glovebox, the email inbox and the bin. When you sell the car, or when a warranty claim asks for proof of servicing, you find you can't prove much.

So a working system needs three parts:

  • Countdowns for fixed dates, so you see them coming weeks ahead, not the day before.
  • "Days since" counters for time-based checks, so you can see how long it's actually been.
  • One place for records: receipts, odometer readings and what was done.

2. "Days since" streaks for time-based checks

  1. Go to Since, tap + and choose Streak.
  2. Name it for the check: "Tire pressure checked" or "Car washed". Set Started on... to the last time you did it; past dates work.
  3. Turn on elapsed-time reminders, for example after 30 days for tire pressure or 14 days for a wash.
  4. When you do the job, open the streak and tap Restart. The count goes back to zero and the history stays.

This is the same idea as a days since widget: the number climbing is the reminder. If you're not sure when you last did something, the days since calculator gives you the count from a date on a receipt.

3. A monthly habit for the walk-around

Create a Habit called "Car walk-around", set it to monthly, and add a reminder for a time you're usually at home, such as Saturday morning. Ticking it off is the cue to restart the tire pressure streak at the same time.

4. One folder for receipts and readings

Make a "Car" folder in Files or a note in Apple Notes. Each time you pay for a service, add a photo of the receipt with the date and odometer reading in the file name or the note. A simple spreadsheet with columns for date, mileage, what was done and cost works just as well and is easy to hand to a buyer.

Questions

How often should a car be serviced?

Most manufacturers recommend a service once a year or at a set distance, often somewhere around 10,000 miles (16,000 km), whichever comes first. Your service book gives the exact interval for your car, and heavy use such as towing or lots of short trips can shorten it.

Do I need an oil change if I barely drive?

Usually yes. Oil ages even when the car sits, so most manuals give a time limit, often 12 months, alongside the distance. Low-mileage drivers should go by the date.

What maintenance does an electric car need?

No oil changes, but tires, brakes, brake fluid, wipers, cabin filters, the 12-volt battery and often battery coolant still need attention. Registration, inspection and insurance dates apply the same as for any car.

What is the easiest way to remember car registration and insurance renewals?

Put each one in a countdown with reminders a month and a week before, and save it to your calendar. A yearly repeat means you only set it up once.
