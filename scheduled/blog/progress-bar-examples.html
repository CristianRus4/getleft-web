Progress Bar Examples: 12 Kinds for Goals, Time and Money (2027)

Updated November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Most progress bar examples online are about loading screens. This page is about the other kind: the bar that shows how far you are through a goal, a savings target, a project or the year. Below are 12 progress bar examples, what each one is good for, where each one falls short, and three practical ways to make a progress bar yourself: on paper, in a spreadsheet and on your iPhone Home Screen.

First, decide what 100% means

A progress bar is a ratio: done divided by total. The bar only means something if the total is clear. Real-life totals come in four kinds:

  • Count: 14 of 24 books, 70 of 100 km.
  • Amount: $3,200 of a $10,000 emergency fund.
  • Steps: 4 of 7 stages of a kitchen renovation.
  • Time: days gone out of the days in a year, a semester or a training plan.

12 progress bar examples for real life

1. The classic linear bar

Good for: any single goal with a fixed total, like 7,000 of 10,000 dollars saved. Everyone reads it instantly, and length is the easiest visual quantity to judge.

Weak spot: 70% of a reading goal and 70% of a mortgage look identical. Put the real numbers next to it ("7,000 / 10,000") or it says very little.

2. Segmented (stepped) bar

Good for: goals made of distinct steps: a five-chapter thesis, a 12-week course, a move broken into stages. Each block that fills in is a small, visible win.

Weak spot: steps are rarely equal. If chapter 4 is twice the work of the others, the bar overstates your progress after chapter 3. Keep steps roughly the same size, or use a linear bar.

3. Circular ring

Good for: small spaces, like a watch face or a small widget, and goals that reset, like a daily step count. A closed ring feels finished in a way a full bar doesn't.

Weak spot: people judge angles less accurately than lengths, so 60% and 70% look closer on a ring than on a bar. Put the number inside or next to it.

4. Nested rings

Apple's Activity rings in the Fitness app are the best-known example: Move, Exercise and Stand in one compact shape that refills every day.

Good for: two or three related daily goals you want to see together.

Weak spot: past three rings it becomes a target pattern. And because the rings reset daily, they show today, not the trend.

5. Grid of dots or squares

Good for: time. November as 30 squares (8 done on the morning of the 9th), a year as 365 squares, or a life in weeks, about 4,700 squares for 90 years. Each square is one real unit, so you count instead of estimate. If you want the math behind it, see what year progress is and how it's calculated.

Weak spot: it needs room. 365 squares in a small widget get tiny, and a grid shows how much, not whether you're on pace.

6. Stacked bar

Good for: one total fed by several sources. A $10,000 down payment with $3,000 from savings, $2,000 from a bonus and $1,500 from a side job: one bar, three shades, 65% in total.

Weak spot: the middle segments are hard to compare by eye. Keep it to three or four parts and label each one.

7. Thermometer

Good for: shared goals shown to a group: a school fundraiser, a team savings pot, a charity walk. It fills from the bottom toward a goal line and reads from across a room.

Weak spot: it takes vertical space and suggests one fixed target. If the goal moves, redraw it instead of drawing past the top.

8. Milestone bar

A linear bar with labeled marks along it: 5K, 10K, half marathon, marathon. Or a $1,000 starter fund, then one month of expenses, then three.

Good for: long goals where the finish is far away. The next mark is always closer than the end, which keeps the bar from feeling stuck.

Weak spot: too many labels crowd it. Three to five milestones is plenty.

9. Countdown bar that empties

The same bar in reverse: it starts full and drains as a date gets closer. "30% of the semester left."

Good for: deadlines, where the thing that matters is time remaining.

Weak spot: a draining bar reads like a warning. For something you're looking forward to, such as a trip, a bar that fills toward the day feels better.

10. Text bar made of ▓ and ░

▓▓▓▓▓▓▓░░░ 70%

Good for: anywhere that only takes text: a note, a message, a social bio, a spreadsheet cell. Ten characters give 10% steps; twenty give 5% steps.

Weak spot: it's coarse, and some fonts draw the two characters at different widths, so the bar can look uneven. Use a monospaced font when you can.

11. Percentage only

86%

Sometimes the number is the bar. That's roughly how much of 2027 is done by the end of November 9.

Good for: tight spots like the Lock Screen, and figures people already understand, like year progress.

Weak spot: a percentage hides scale. 86% of a 10 km run and 86% of a 10-year loan feel very different, and the number alone doesn't say which.

12. Pace bar: progress against time

Work
Time

Two bars stacked: one for the work done, one for the time used.

Good for: any goal with a deadline. If the time bar is ahead of the work bar, you're behind; if the work bar leads, you have slack. Say your goal is 24 books this year: in early November you're about 86% through the year, so you'd want to be near 20 books.

Weak spot: it assumes steady progress. Goals with a slow start or a big final push, like exam prep or a training plan, will look behind for a while by design.

Whichever you pick, three rules keep it honest: show the real numbers beside the bar, don't quietly change the total, and update it often enough that it moves.

How to make a progress bar: three practical ways

On paper or in a bullet journal

  1. Pick the total and divide it into 10 or 20 equal parts. A $5,000 goal becomes 20 boxes of $250.
  2. Draw the boxes in a row for a segmented bar, or as a 10 by 10 grid where each square is 1%.
  3. Shade a box each time you cross a step. Writing the date inside turns the bar into a log.
  4. Mark two or three milestones in a different color.

Shading a box by hand feels good. The catch is that the bar only exists where the notebook is.

In a spreadsheet

Google Sheets: with your current amount in B2 and the target in C2, =SPARKLINE(B2,{"charttype","bar";"max",C2}) draws a bar inside the cell. Add a "color1" option to change its color.

Excel: select the cells, then Home → Conditional Formatting → Data Bars. By default the bars scale to the largest value in the selection, so open Manage Rules → Edit Rule and set the maximum to your target number.

Text bar in either: =REPT("▓",ROUND(MIN(B2/C2,1)*10,0))&REPT("░",10-ROUND(MIN(B2/C2,1)*10,0)) builds a 10-character bar that stops at full even if you pass the target.

Questions

How do you make a progress bar in Google Sheets?

Put your current amount in one cell (say B2) and the target in another (C2), then use =SPARKLINE(B2,{"charttype","bar";"max",C2}). It draws a bar inside the cell that fills as B2 grows. For a text bar, REPT repeats ▓ and ░ characters in proportion to B2/C2.

How do you calculate the percentage for a progress bar?

Divide what's done by the total and multiply by 100. $7,200 saved toward $10,000 is 72%. For time, divide the days that have passed by the days in the period: November 9 is day 313 of 2027, so the year is about 86% done by the end of that day. The percentage of the year calculator does this for any date.

Is a progress ring better than a progress bar?

Neither wins everywhere. A bar is easier to read precisely and to compare with other bars, because people judge length better than angle. A ring fits small spaces and suits goals that reset, like a daily step count. If precision matters, use a bar; if space is tight, use a ring with the number inside.

Can you put a progress bar on your iPhone Home Screen?

Yes, with a widget. Apple's Fitness widget shows your Activity rings, but for your own goals you need a widget app. In Left, an Aim widget shows a progress bar toward a target, Ahead Bars and Ahead Circle show time until a date, and the Time Left grids show the year, month or day. The goal progress tracker setup guide walks through it.

How do you type a text progress bar like ▓▓▓░░?

Use the dark shade character ▓ for done and the light shade ░ for remaining, or the full block █ for a heavier look. Copy them from this page. On iPhone, you can save a ready-made bar as a text replacement in Settings → General → Keyboard → Text Replacement. Ten characters make a bar with 10% steps; add the percentage after it so the bar is never ambiguous.
