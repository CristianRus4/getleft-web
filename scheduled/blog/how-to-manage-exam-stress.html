How to Manage Exam Stress: A Plan for the Weeks, the Night Before and the Morning Of

Updated December 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Some exam stress is useful. It's what gets you to open the book. The trouble starts when it stops pointing at anything: a vague sense that there's too much to learn and not enough time, which makes it harder to start, which makes the time shorter. The fix for that kind of stress is rarely "relax". It's a plan small enough to believe in.

This guide covers how to manage exam stress in three stretches: the weeks before, the last few days, and the morning of the exam. It's practical advice for students, not medical advice. If stress is affecting your sleep, eating or mood for weeks, talk to a doctor, a counselor or your school's student support service.

Why exam stress grows when the date is vague

Stress feeds on uncertainty. "Finals are in a few weeks" is uncertain on two counts: how long you have, and what you need to do with it. Your brain fills both gaps with the worst case. Making both numbers concrete takes a lot of the pressure out:

  • How long: "23 days, of which 17 are study days."
  • What to do: "6 topics, 3 sessions each, 18 sessions."

Eighteen sessions in seventeen days is a plan. "Everything, soon" is a feeling.

Weeks before: turn the countdown into sessions

  1. Count the real days. Take the days until the exam and subtract the days you can't study: work shifts, other exams, a family event. Be honest. That's your study-day count.
  2. List the topics. Use the syllabus or past papers, not memory. Mark each one red (don't get it), amber (shaky) or green (fine).
  3. Size the work. Give red topics three sessions, amber two, green one. A session is 25 to 50 minutes of focused work.
  4. Fit sessions to days. Divide total sessions by study days. If it's more than about four a day, cut scope now: drop the least likely topics or lower the bar on green ones. Deciding this in week one feels far better than discovering it the night before.
  5. Leave the last two days light. Plan them for review and past papers only, not new material.

Worked example: an exam in 21 days, 4 of them unavailable, leaves 17 study days. Eight topics (3 red, 3 amber, 2 green) need 9 + 6 + 2 = 17 sessions. One session a day, plus the light last two days. That's an easy plan, and knowing it's easy is the point.

During study sessions: lower the cost of starting

Most exam stress peaks just before you start, not while you're working. A few things make starting cheaper:

  • Know the first task before you sit down. "Do question 3 of the 2024 paper" beats "revise chemistry".
  • Use a timer. Committing to 25 minutes is easier than committing to "until I understand it". Left's Focus timer (part of Left Premium) runs 25-minute sessions with breaks in between, or use the Clock app's timer.
  • Phone face down, out of reach. Checking messages mid-session restarts the hardest part: getting back in.
  • Practice under exam conditions. Timed past papers turn the exam room into a familiar place. Familiar is less stressful.
  • Test, don't reread. Recalling answers from memory is uncomfortable, but it's what builds the confidence you'll need in the room.

Protect the basics

When time feels short, sleep, food and movement are the first things students cut. They're also what your memory and mood run on.

  • Sleep. Sleep is when the day's learning settles. Trading it for late-night cramming usually costs more than it gains. Keep a steady bedtime, even in exam week. Our sleep calculator helps you pick one that matches your wake-up time.
  • Movement. A short walk between sessions resets attention and takes the edge off nerves.
  • Food and water. Regular meals beat a day of snacks and energy drinks.
  • One thing that isn't studying. A game, a call with a friend, a show. Time off is part of the plan, not a break from it.

The night before

  • Stop learning new material. Review your summary notes or flashcards for an hour, then stop.
  • Pack your bag. ID, pens, calculator, water, anything your exam allows. Check the room and start time.
  • Write down what's worrying you. Putting worries on paper before bed can make them easier to set aside.
  • Go to bed at your normal time. Not earlier to "make up for it", which often means lying awake.

The morning of the exam

  • Leave early. Arriving with 20 minutes to spare beats sprinting in. If you use Left, turn on Get ready for the start for the exam date and a Live Activity counts down the final hours on your Lock Screen, so you always know how long you have.
  • Eat something. Even if you're not hungry.
  • Skip the corridor quiz. Other students' last-minute questions mostly spread panic.
  • Breathe slowly before you start. A few slow breaths, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath, help settle the body.
  • Read the paper first. Start with a question you can answer. Early marks build momentum.

After each exam

Don't do a post-mortem with friends in the hallway. Note anything you'd do differently in one line, then turn to the next exam's countdown. The plan resets; so can you.

Questions

How can I reduce stress before an exam?

Make the time and the work concrete. Count your real study days, list topics by how well you know them, and fit study sessions into the days you have. A plan you can see is less stressful than a vague deadline.

Is a countdown to my exam stressful?

It can be if there's no plan behind it. Paired with a session plan, a countdown reads as progress. Show it in weeks while the exam is far off and switch to days in the final stretch.

How many hours should I study per day before an exam?

There's no single number. Many students do well with three to four focused sessions of 25 to 50 minutes a day, with breaks, rather than long unfocused hours. Plan from the topics you need to cover and the days you have.

What should I do the night before an exam?

Review summary notes briefly, pack your bag, check the room and time, write down any worries, and go to bed at your usual time. Avoid learning new material.

When should I get help for exam stress?

If stress affects your sleep, eating or mood for weeks, or you feel unable to cope, talk to a doctor, a counselor or your school's student support service.
