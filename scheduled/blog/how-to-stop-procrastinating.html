How to Stop Procrastinating: Find Your Reason, Then Fix That

Updated January 14, 2027 · 9 min read

You're not putting off the task. You're putting off how the task makes you feel. Opening the tax form means feeling confused. Starting the essay means feeling judged by a page that isn't good yet. Cleaning the garage means feeling swamped before you've lifted a box. Scrolling for twenty minutes makes that feeling go away, for twenty minutes.

That's the view many psychologists who study procrastination now take: it's mostly a way of managing a bad mood in the short term, not a failure to manage time. It's also why "just start" works on some days and not others: different delays have different causes, and each cause has its own fix.

This guide gives you a quick way to diagnose which of five reasons is behind the thing you're avoiding right now, a fix with a worked example for each, a five-step protocol for today, and a small setup on your iPhone that makes the next delay less likely.

The 30-second diagnosis

Take the one task you keep sliding past and finish this sentence honestly: "I don't want to start this because…" Whatever comes out almost always fits one of five reasons.

ReasonWhat it sounds likeThe fix
It's unclear"I don't even know where to begin."Name the next physical action
It's too big"It'll take all day and I don't have all day."Shrink it to a 10-minute version
It's boring"Ugh. I'll do it later."Pair it with something you like, or make it a game
It's scary"It has to be good, and it won't be."Lower the bar with a bad first draft

Two reasons often stack. If more than one fits, fix them in the order of the table. You can't shrink a task you haven't named, and a small, clear task is usually less scary than a vague, huge one.

Reason 1: The task is unclear

Watch out for verbs like "sort out", "deal with", "look into" and "work on". They describe an outcome, not something your hands can do. Your brain looks at "sort out car insurance", can't find a first move, and goes looking for something easier.

The fix: write down the next physical action. The test is simple: could you do it in the next five minutes without having to decide anything else?

  • "Sort out car insurance" becomes "Find last year's renewal email and copy the policy number into a note."
  • "Deal with the dentist" becomes "Call the practice at 9:15 and ask for the first free Tuesday."

Once the first action is done, the second is usually obvious. You're not planning the whole project, just the next step.

Reason 2: The task is too big

A big task asks you to commit to the whole thing before you start. That's what makes it heavy. The trick is to stop committing to the whole thing.

The fix: make a 10-minute version and give yourself full permission to stop when the timer ends. Ten minutes is short enough that your brain can't argue with it, and long enough to get real work done.

Worked example. "Clean the garage" has been on your list since spring. The 10-minute version: "Clear the shelf by the door. Rubbish in one bag, keep pile on the floor." Set a timer, do only that, and stop when it rings if you want to. Most of the time you'll do another round, because starting was the hard part.

This is the same idea as the Pomodoro method, just with a shorter first round. If the full method appeals, our roundup of time management practices covers it alongside time blocking and the others.

Reason 3: The task is boring

Some tasks aren't hard or unclear. They're just dull: filing receipts, updating a spreadsheet, replying to routine emails. Boredom is a feeling too, and a surprisingly strong one.

The fix: borrow some interest from somewhere else. Three ways that work:

  • Pair it. Allow yourself something you enjoy only while you do the dull thing: a podcast you save for filing, a good coffee for admin hour. Researchers call this temptation bundling.
  • Race it. Count something and try to beat your last score. Forty receipts in a shoebox: how many can you file in one 25-minute round with a podcast on? You file 23, so next month the target is 24. It's a silly game, and that's the point.
  • Do it next to someone. Working beside another person, in a room or on a video call, makes dull work easier to keep going with. Our guide to body doubling covers apps and low-tech ways to do it.

Reason 4: The task is scary

This one hides well, because it rarely feels like fear. It feels like "I'll do it when I can give it proper attention". Underneath is a worry about being judged, or a standard so high that any real attempt falls short of it.

The fix: separate making from judging. Use a bad first draft rule: the first version is allowed to be bad, nobody will see it, and its only job is to exist.

Worked example. You need to write a cover letter. Instead of "write the cover letter", give yourself 15 minutes to write the worst acceptable version. Bullet points are fine. Typos are fine. "I'm good at this because [something]" is fine. When the timer ends, you have something to improve, and editing a bad draft is far less frightening than facing an empty page.

It works for dreaded messages too: draft the email as three blunt bullet points, then make them polite once the hard thinking is done.

Reason 5: The deadline is too far away to feel

A deadline in five weeks doesn't feel real. Your brain treats it as "later", and later stays later until the night before, when the panic finally gives you the push you were missing.

The fix: bring the deadline closer, in one of two ways.

Make the deadline visible

A date in your calendar is something you look up. A countdown on your Lock Screen is something you see every time you pick up your phone. "23 days to the thesis deadline", seen many times a day, slowly turns "later" into a number that keeps going down.

Today: a 5-step emergency protocol

If you're reading this instead of doing something, do this now.

  1. Pick one task. Only one. Write it down so it stops floating.
  2. Name the reason. Unclear, too big, boring, scary, or too far away. Thirty seconds, no more.
  3. Write the first action, sized to 10 minutes. Apply the fix for your reason: a physical action, a smaller version, a podcast, a bad draft or a fake deadline.
  4. Clear the space. Close the tabs you don't need. Put your phone face down or in another room, or turn on an iPhone Focus mode that silences notifications.
  5. Start a 10-minute timer and do only that. When it ends, choose: another round, or stop. Both count. You started, and starting was the job.

Stuck again halfway? Go back to step 2. The reason often changes once you've started.

When it's more than procrastination

Everyone procrastinates. But if putting things off is constant, spreads across work, home and things you enjoy, and none of these fixes make a dent, it may be a sign of something else. ADHD often shows up as trouble starting tasks, and depression and burnout can drain the energy that starting needs. A doctor or mental health professional can help you work out what's going on. Questions

Why do I procrastinate even on things I want to do?

Wanting the result doesn't make the first step feel good. A project you care about is often the scariest kind, because doing it badly would matter. Use the bad first draft rule and a 10-minute start.

Is procrastination just laziness?

Usually not. Lazy means not wanting to do anything, while procrastinators often work hard at everything except the one task. It's more often a way of avoiding an uncomfortable feeling, which is why the fixes target the feeling.

What's the fastest way to stop procrastinating right now?

Write down the next physical action, set a 10-minute timer and do only that, with permission to stop when it rings. Starting is the hardest part, and a small enough start is hard to refuse.

Does the Pomodoro Technique help with procrastination?

It helps most when a task feels too big or boring, because it swaps "finish this" for "work for 25 minutes". If 25 minutes feels like too much, start with 10. It does less for unclear or scary tasks, which need their own fixes first.

Can an app stop me procrastinating?

No app does the task for you. What an app can do is make deadlines visible, put a timer one tap away and track whether you started, which removes some of the friction and some of the "later".
