Home Renovation Timeline: Phases, Lead Times and Where Delays Hide

Updated January 10, 2027 · 10 min read

Renovations rarely run late at random. They run late in the same five places: a decision nobody made in time, an approval that took longer than expected, a cabinet order that was placed too late, one trade slipping and pushing every trade behind it, and whatever turns up behind the walls. If you know where those places are, you can plan around most of them before anyone picks up a hammer.

Below: the phases in order with rough lengths, where each one hides its delay, a bathroom and a kitchen week by week, buffer rules, living through the work, and a small iPhone setup for the finish date and the budget.

How long does a renovation take?

It varies a lot by project and location, so treat these as rough ranges, not promises. A full bathroom refit often takes about 3 to 6 weeks on site. A kitchen often takes about 6 to 12 weeks on site. Extensions, structural changes and whole-house work usually run to months.

The part people forget is the time before the site work. Designing, quotes, approvals and deliveries can take as long as the building itself. Your contractor's estimate is the best guide for your project; the ranges below help you check it.

The phases of a renovation, in order

Every renovation moves through the same sequence. Some phases shrink to a day on a small job and some disappear (not every project needs an approval or an inspection), but the order holds. Lengths are rough, for a single room, and vary a lot by project and location.

  • Decide and budget. Write the job in one sentence ("replace the bathroom, same layout") and set a total with a contingency line. Moving a toilet, a sink or a wall changes the price and the timeline far more than changing tiles.
  • Design and quotes. Ask each contractor for a start date and an on-site duration, not just a price. A cheaper quote that starts in four months may finish later than one that starts in three weeks.
  • Snagging. Walk the room with your contractor and list every small fault. Agree a date for the fixes before the final payment.

Where renovation delays hide

Decisions made too late

The tiler can't start until the tiles are chosen, and the electrician can't place sockets until the cabinet layout is final. Every open decision is a future pause. The rule that prevents most of this: no demolition until every finish is chosen and every long-lead item has a confirmed delivery date. It feels slow. It saves more time than anything else here.

Approvals

Whether you need permission, and how long it takes, depends entirely on where you live. Structural changes, extensions, moving gas or drains, some electrical work and heritage or shared buildings are common triggers. Check with your local authority's building or planning office before you set any dates, and don't book trades for dates that depend on an approval you don't have yet.

Long-lead items

Made-to-order windows and doors, custom cabinets, bespoke joinery and some appliances can take weeks or months to arrive. Ask the lead time, write down the promised delivery date, and order these first. Plan where they'll be stored if they arrive early; a kitchen in boxes needs a lot of floor space.

Trades booked in sequence

Plumber, electrician, plasterer, tiler and decorator are usually booked one after another. If the plasterer loses three days, the tiler's slot may be gone and the next one weeks away. Ask your contractor which trade is hardest to rebook, and protect that slot.

Surprises behind walls

Rot, old wiring, leaking pipes, uneven floors. Older homes may also contain hazardous materials such as asbestos or lead paint; have a professional check before demolition and follow your local guidance. You can't plan the surprise, but you can plan for one: that's what the buffer is for.

A bathroom, week by week

A full refit with the same layout. Yours will differ, but the shape is typical.

  • Weeks −8 to −1 (before site work): scope and budget, two or three quotes, every finish chosen, vanity, shower screen and tiles ordered with confirmed delivery dates. Approvals checked.
  • Week 1: strip out the old suite, tiles and flooring. Fix whatever turns up. Plumbing and electrical rough-in. Inspection if required.
  • Week 2: boards or plaster on walls, floor levelled, waterproofing applied and left to cure.
  • Week 3: tiling and grouting. Ceiling and walls painted.
  • Week 4: toilet, basin, shower and fittings connected, lights and fan in, sealant applied. Snagging walk-through, then fixes.

If it's your only bathroom, the plan has to say where everyone showers for those four weeks.

A kitchen, week by week

A larger project: a kitchen with new cabinets, stone countertops (worktops) and the sink moved along the same wall.

  • Weeks −12 to −1: design and quotes, then the cabinet order, often the longest lead time. Appliances and sink chosen first so the cabinets fit them. Approvals checked if a wall, gas or drains are involved.
  • Week 1: old kitchen out, temporary kitchen set up elsewhere.
  • Weeks 2 and 3: structural work if any, then plumbing, gas and electrical rough-in. Inspections where required.
  • Week 4: walls patched and plastered, flooring laid if it runs under the cabinets.
  • Week 5: cabinets installed. Countertops measured on site (templated) once the base cabinets are level.
  • Weeks 6 and 7: the gap people don't expect. Stone countertops are made after templating, which commonly takes one to a few weeks. Painting happens now.
  • Week 8: countertops fitted, then sink, hob, splashback and appliances connected.
  • Week 9: snagging and final cleanup.

Roughly nine weeks on site after up to three months of preparation, assuming nothing goes wrong. Hence the buffer.

Buffer rules for time and money

  • Money: a common rule of thumb is to keep 10 to 20% of the budget as contingency. Lean towards the top end for older homes and structural work.
  • Time: add about a week for every month of planned site work. A nine-week kitchen becomes an eleven-week kitchen on your calendar.
  • Count working days: contractors schedule in working days, and public holidays vanish from the plan. The business days calculator shows how many really sit between start and finish.
  • Don't book anything on the finish date. Not the end of a rental, not family arriving for the holidays. Put those after the buffered date.

The days between two dates tool helps sanity-check a quote: if the stated duration and the start and end dates don't agree, ask why.

Living through a renovation

Most of the stress isn't the schedule. It's living in the house while it runs. Plan these before week 1:

  • Where you'll cook. Set up a temporary kitchen in another room: kettle, microwave or slow cooker, a small fridge, a washing-up bowl. Budget for some takeaways.
  • Where you'll wash. If the only bathroom is out, arrange showers with family, friends, neighbors or a gym in advance, and ask your contractor which days you'll have no working toilet.
  • Where you'll sleep. Dust travels. Seal the work area with plastic sheeting and keep bedroom doors closed. For floor sanding, demolition or days without power or water, staying elsewhere is often worth it.
  • Kids and pets. A site has tools, nails and open floors. Keep them out completely, behind a door that closes. Pets may be happier with a friend on the loudest days.
  • Access. Agree working hours, who has a key, and where trades park and deliveries go. Move anything fragile out of the way.

When the date slips

It probably will, at least once. Ask three questions: what caused it, which later trade it pushes, and whether anything booked after the finish date is now at risk. Then update the finish date in your plan and on your phone. A countdown to a date everyone knows is wrong teaches you to ignore it.

A short weekly check-in with your contractor helps: what got done, what's next, what's waiting on you. Questions

How long does a kitchen renovation take?

Often about 6 to 12 weeks on site, plus several weeks to a few months of design, quotes and waiting for cabinets beforehand. It varies a lot by project and location, so ask your contractor for both the start date and the on-site duration.

How long does a bathroom renovation take?

A full refit with the same layout often takes about 3 to 6 weeks on site. Moving the toilet, shower or drains, structural work or waiting for an approval can add weeks.

What should you do first when renovating a house?

Set the scope and the budget, including a contingency line, then check whether your work needs approval with your local authority. After that, get quotes and order the long-lead items before booking demolition.

How much contingency should you add to a renovation?

A common rule of thumb is 10 to 20% of the budget, towards the top end for older homes or structural work. Add time contingency too, about a week for every month of site work.

Why do renovations take longer than planned?

Mostly late decisions, approvals, long-lead items arriving late, trades booked in sequence where one slip moves the rest, and surprises found behind walls. Choosing every finish before demolition removes the most common cause.
