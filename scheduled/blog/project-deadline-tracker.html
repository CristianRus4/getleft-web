Project Deadline Tracker: How to See Deadline Collisions Before They Hit, on iPhone

Updated January 18, 2027 · 8 min read

If you run one project, tracking its deadline is easy. You know the date, you work toward it, you hit it or you don't. The trouble starts with three or four projects at once. Each deadline looks fine on its own. Then two of them land in the same week, a third has a milestone due the Monday after, and suddenly nothing is fine.

That's the real risk when you juggle projects: not one deadline, but deadlines bunching up. A useful project deadline tracker is built to show you the bunching weeks in advance, while you can still move things. This guide covers the map, a ten-minute weekly review, asking for more time, and which tools fit which job, including the iPhone app we make.

Why several fine deadlines add up to a bad week

Most tools show deadlines one project at a time. The client project lives in one board, the internal report in another, the side project in your head. Each one gets planned as if it were the only thing you're doing.

Work also doesn't spread evenly. Most of the effort lands in the week or two before a deadline, whatever the plan said. So the load you feel isn't the date itself but its shadow, the stretch just before it. Two deadlines ten days apart can still cast overlapping shadows.

The fix is to put every deadline, from every project, on one line.

Build a deadline map for the next 8 weeks

A deadline map is a list of the next 6 to 8 weeks, one row per week, with every deadline and milestone written in the week it falls. Paper or a phone note is fine.

  1. List every active project, work and personal.
  2. For each one, write the final deadline and 2 to 4 milestones before it.
  3. Put each item in its week. Mark anything with a fixed external date (a launch, a filing, a talk) so you know it can't move.
  4. Count the items per week.

Here's a map for someone running four projects: a client website relaunch, a quarterly board report, a grant application and a conference talk.

Spot collisions while they're still cheap

A collision is any week with a fixed deadline plus another real milestone, or three items of any kind. In the map above, weeks 4 and 6 are collisions, and week 5 is close.

You have three ways to fix one, roughly in order of preference:

  • Pull work forward. Move milestones into the empty weeks. The talk outline can happen in week 1. The grant budget can move to week 2.
  • Cut scope. Does the board report need a new section this quarter, or can it reuse last quarter's structure?
  • Move a deadline. Only the ones that aren't fixed, and only early. More on that below.

The earlier you see a collision, the more of these options you have. Six weeks out, all three work. Three days out, you're left with long evenings.

Treat milestones as mini-deadlines

A project with only a final date gives you one warning, and it comes too late. Milestones give you earlier warnings. If the staging review slips in week 6, you know the launch in week 7 is at risk while there's still a week to react.

Good milestones are finished things, not activities. "Content in" is a milestone. "Work on content" isn't, because you can never tell whether it's done. Two to four per project is plenty.

Put buffers where they protect you

Keep two dates for anything that matters: the real deadline and your own, a few working days earlier. You aim for your own date. The gap between the two is the buffer, and it absorbs the sick day, the late feedback, the file that won't export.

Give each project its own buffer rather than one shared cushion at the end of the month. Shared buffers get used by whichever project complains first. Count the buffer in working days, not calendar days, because a weekend in the middle is not three days of slack. The business days calculator does that arithmetic.

Days left beats dates for urgency

Dates are good for planning and for talking to other people: "We launch on the 14th." They're bad at creating urgency, because "the 14th" asks you to do mental math every time you see it.

"23 days left" doesn't. And "16 working days left" is even more honest. Keep dates in your plan and your calendar, and put days left wherever you look during the day. The deadline countdown calculator gives you both numbers for any date.

The 10-minute weekly deadline review

Same time every week, such as Friday after lunch.

  1. Minutes 1 to 3: add anything new. New projects, new dates someone mentioned in a meeting, milestones you forgot.
  2. Minutes 4 to 6: check the next 3 weeks. Count items per week. Mark any collision.
  3. Minutes 7 to 8: check milestones due this week. Done, on track or slipping. A slipping milestone is your early warning.
  4. Minutes 9 to 10: decide one fix. Pull something forward, cut something, or write the email asking to move a date.

This review is about dates only. For the wider weekly routine across several projects (priorities, next actions, a daily reset), see how to keep track of multiple projects.

How to renegotiate a deadline early

Asking for more time two weeks out is a scheduling conversation. Asking the day before is an apology. Most people would rather hear early.

Keep the message short and give the other person something to say yes to:

  • What: which deliverable.
  • New date: one specific date, not "a bit later".
  • Why, in one line: "It lands the same week as the grant submission."
  • What they get now: a partial version, an earlier review, or a firm commitment to the new date.

For example: "The staging review is due the 3rd, the same week as a fixed grant submission. Could we move it to the 7th? I can send the homepage and pricing pages on the 3rd so you can start reviewing." Then update your map the moment they reply.

Questions

What is a project deadline tracker?

A project deadline tracker is one place that shows every deadline and milestone across all your projects, so you can see which weeks are overloaded. It can be a list on paper, a project management tool or a countdown app on your phone.

How do I track deadlines for several projects at once?

Put every deadline and milestone from every project on one list of the next 6 to 8 weeks, one row per week. Count the items in each week, and treat any week with a fixed deadline plus another milestone as a collision to fix early.

How much buffer should I add before a deadline?

Set your own target a few working days before the real deadline, and give each project its own buffer. Projects with outside dependencies, such as client feedback, need more.

When should I ask to move a deadline?

As soon as you see a collision, ideally weeks ahead rather than days. Name the deliverable, propose one specific new date, give a one-line reason and offer something now, such as a partial version.

Is Left a project management app?
