Habit Tracker for ADHD: How to Set Up One on iPhone That You'll Keep Using

Updated November 9, 2026 · 10 min read

The usual story goes like this. You download a habit tracker on a good evening, add eight habits, pick colors, set reminders for each one, and feel briefly organized. Three days later you miss one. A week later the app is in a folder. A month later you download a different one and do it all again.

If you have ADHD, or just recognize yourself in that loop, the problem usually isn't the habits and it isn't you. Most habit trackers are built for people who already find routine easy: they reward unbroken chains, assume you'll remember to open the app, and front-load all the effort into setup day. This guide covers why that design works against an ADHD brain, what a tracker setup that works with it looks like on iPhone, a few apps worth trying, and a step-by-step setup in Left.

One note before we start: this is practical advice about apps and routines, not medical advice. If daily tasks feel impossible no matter what system you use, that's worth raising with a clinician.

Why most habit trackers fail ADHD brains

None of these are character flaws. They're common experiences that a lot of people with ADHD describe, and they collide with how most trackers are designed.

The streak punishes one miss

A streak counter is a single number that drops to zero the moment you skip a day. Twenty-nine good days and one bad one looks the same as never having started. For anyone prone to all-or-nothing thinking, that zero reads as "I failed," and the easiest way to stop feeling that is to stop opening the app.

Too many habits at once

Setup day is the most motivated you'll ever be, so it's tempting to track everything: water, reading, flossing, journaling, exercise, bedtime. By day four the list is mostly unchecked, and a screen full of misses is a reason to look away.

Out of sight, out of mind

If a habit lives inside an app, it exists only when you remember to open that app. Many people with ADHD find that what isn't in front of them effectively doesn't exist. Reminders don't fully fix this: a notification that arrives mid-task gets swiped away, and the habit goes with it.

Setup fatigue and fading novelty

Apps that ask for categories, icons, colors, goals and notes for every habit burn your energy before you've done anything. And a new app is interesting for a week or two. When the novelty fades, the app needs to keep working without your enthusiasm.

What an ADHD-friendly habit tracker setup looks like

These work in almost any app. Make the tracker do the remembering, and keep the bar low enough that a bad day still counts.

1. Track one or two habits, not ten

Start with one habit you actually care about. Add a second only after the first has run for a couple of weeks without much effort. A short list fits on one widget, gets checked at a glance, and is rarely all red.

2. Set a tiny floor

Define each habit by the smallest version that still counts. "Read 1 page," not "read for 30 minutes." "Put on running shoes," not "run 5 km." "Take meds" is already tiny; keep it that way. On good days you'll do more. On bad days the floor keeps the habit alive, and that's what builds it.

3. Put it where your eyes already go

Your Lock Screen, your Home Screen's first page, your watch face. A widget that shows today's habit, unchecked, is a reminder that never times out. If you can log from the widget itself, even better: seeing it and doing something about it happen in the same second.

4. Use reminders that stay

Pick reminder times that match real moments ("after I sit down at my desk," "after dinner"), and use more than one if a habit has a long window. For habits you tend to forget late in the day, a persistent reminder that stays on screen until you log is far harder to ignore than a banner.

5. Measure consistency, not perfection

Look for a tracker that shows something besides the streak: a completion grid, days completed versus missed, or a consistency score. Five days out of seven is a habit taking shape. A measure that reflects that, instead of resetting to zero, keeps a missed day from undoing your motivation. Decide now what you'll do after a miss: nothing special, just log the next one.

6. Log with one tap, where you are

Every step between doing a habit and recording it is a chance to forget. Logging from a widget, a watch complication or Siri removes the "I'll log it later" gap. If you wear an Apple Watch, a tap on the wrist right after the walk is about as easy as it gets.

7. Let someone else see it

Many people with ADHD find tasks easier with another person around, which is the idea behind body doubling. A lighter version for habits is letting one friend follow your progress. You don't need anyone to nag you; knowing someone can see the habit is often enough. Keep it to one or two habits, so it stays encouraging rather than stressful.

2. Streaks

A long-standing iPhone and Apple Watch habit app that deliberately caps how many tasks you can have, which suits the "fewer habits" rule. Habits linked to the Health app can complete themselves.

Best for: a short daily checklist without a subscription.

Pros: enforced short list; Health auto-completion; polished Watch app.

Cons: as the name says, it centers the streak, which can sting after a miss; no free version.

3. HabitKit

HabitKit shows each habit as a grid of colored squares, one per day, so you see the overall pattern rather than one number. A missed square is just a gap in a mostly filled grid.

Best for: people who are motivated by seeing a pattern fill in.

Pros: clean, visual, quick to set up.

Cons: grids reward the long run, so the first week has little to look at; it shows progress more than it prompts you.

4. Tiimo

Tiimo is a visual daily planner designed with neurodivergent people in mind. Routines are laid out as colorful blocks on a timeline, which helps if the hard part is knowing when to start.

Best for: building habits into a visual day plan.

Pros: built around visual structure; good for routines with several steps.

Cons: a planner first, so long-term habit stats aren't its focus; much of it is subscription-based.

5. Habitica

Habitica turns habits and to-dos into a role-playing game: you earn experience and gold for doing things and lose health for skipping them, and you can join a party with friends.

Best for: people who stick with games longer than with checklists.

Pros: playful; built-in social accountability; available beyond iPhone.

Cons: setup is heavy; the game can become the hobby instead of the habit; penalties may feel like the punishing streak in another form.

How to set up an ADHD-friendly habit tracker in Left

This takes about five minutes. Do it once, then leave the setup alone for two weeks.

  1. Pick one habit and its floor. Write the smallest version: "Read 1 page," "Stretch for one minute," "Take meds."
  2. Create it. Open Since, tap +, and type the habit to find a ready-made template, or choose Build from scratch. Use the floor as the name, so the bar is what you see.
  3. Choose a realistic schedule. Daily habits can run every day, on weekdays, on specific days, or a number of days per week. "4 days a week" leaves room for bad days without counting them as failures.
  4. Turn on Get reminders. Add up to 4 reminders, each with its own days and time. Tie them to moments you already have, like lunch or when you get home.
  5. Turn on Get a persistent reminder. Set Before what time? to your real cutoff, such as 10 pm. If the habit still isn't logged, a Live Activity appears an hour before and stays on your Lock Screen.
  6. Add the Habit widget. Put it on your Home Screen's first page in the Text layout, so you can tap today's circle to log, and add the Lock Screen rectangular version too. The Lock Screen widget guide shows how.
  7. Add it to your watch face. If you wear an Apple Watch, add the Habit complication for this habit, so logging is one tap from your wrist.
  8. Watch habit strength, not just the streak. Open the habit after a week and look at habit strength and the completion grid. That's the honest picture of how it's going.
  9. Optional: add one friend. With Left Premium, a friend can follow your habit. Accountability with friends explains the setup.

A two-week plan that keeps it small

  • Days 1 to 7: one habit, the floor only. If you do more, great, but the log counts either way.
  • Day 8: check habit strength. If it's mostly filled in, keep going. If it's mostly empty, make the floor smaller or move the reminder to a better moment. Don't add anything yet.
  • Day 14: if the first habit runs without much thought, add a second, set up the same way. If it doesn't, stay with one.
  • Any time it feels like a chore: archive the habit to pause it instead of deleting it or quitting the app. Archived days count as skipped, and your history stays.

If your mornings or evenings are where habits fall apart, pair this with a visible sense of time: ADHD time blindness on iPhone covers widgets that make the day's hours easier to see. And if nothing sticks despite a small, visible setup, that's useful information to bring to a doctor or ADHD specialist, not a sign you need a better app.

Questions

What is the best habit tracker for ADHD?

The best one is the one you can see without opening it and that doesn't punish a missed day. Look for Lock Screen and Home Screen widgets, one-tap logging, persistent reminders and a consistency measure besides the streak. Left, Streaks, HabitKit, Tiimo and Habitica each suit different people.

How many habits should I track if I have ADHD?

Start with one, and add a second only after the first has run for about two weeks without much effort. A short list fits on a single widget and is rarely all unchecked, which keeps you opening it.

Are streaks bad for ADHD?

Not always, but a streak alone can backfire because one missed day resets it to zero. Pair it with something more forgiving, like a completion grid or Left's habit strength, and decide in advance that after a miss you simply log the next day.

How do I stop forgetting to log my habits?

Put the habit where you already look and make logging one tap. A Home Screen or Lock Screen widget you can tap, a watch complication, or a persistent reminder that stays on screen until you log all close the "I'll do it later" gap.

Can a habit tracker help with ADHD symptoms?

A habit tracker can make routines easier to see and remember, but it is not a treatment for ADHD. If daily tasks stay hard despite a simple setup, talk to a doctor or ADHD specialist.
