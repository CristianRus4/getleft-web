30 Goal-Setting Examples, With How to Track Each One

Updated November 9, 2026 · 11 min read

Most lists of goal-setting examples stop at the sentence. "Save 3,000 by September." Good sentence. Then October arrives, and you have no idea whether you're on track, because the goal never had anywhere to live.

This list gives every example in three layers: the vague version most people start with, a well-set version with a date, a number and an action, and how to track it. The tracking layer is the part that keeps a goal alive past week three, and it depends on what kind of goal it is. A savings goal and an alcohol-free goal need very different counters.

The three layers of a useful goal

  1. Vague. A direction, not a goal: "get fit", "save more", "read more". Fine as a starting point, useless as something to check.
  2. Well-set. Add a date, a number and the action that moves the number. "Read 24 books by 31 December, two a month, 20 pages before bed." Now you can tell on any given day whether you're ahead or behind.
  3. Tracked. Pick the counter that matches the shape of the goal, and put it somewhere you'll see it without opening anything.

If you want the full framework for layer two, our SMART goals setup guide walks through it. This page is about the examples, and about layer three.

The five ways to track a goal

Almost every personal goal fits one of five shapes. Get the shape right and tracking takes seconds a day. Get it wrong and you end up ticking a box that says nothing about progress.

Goal shapeThe question it answersExamples
Count-up numberHow many so far?Books read, money saved, km run, study hours
Count-down numberHow much is left?Debt, weight to lose, daily screen time
Repeated actionDid I do it today, or this week?Gym three times a week, daily stretch, Sunday budget check
Days without / days sinceHow long has it been?Alcohol-free, smoke-free, no takeaway
A date to reachHow long until?A race, a move, an exam, a trip

You can use any tool that offers the same shapes. The point is to match the counter to the goal.

The race goal is a good example of pairing. The countdown keeps race day real, and the strength habit or a km Aim is what actually gets you there. A date on its own tells you time is passing. It doesn't tell you what to do on Tuesday.

Notice the last row. Some quitting goals track better as a positive action than as days without. "Phone out of the bedroom" is something you do, so it's a habit. For alcohol or smoking, a doctor or a local stop-smoking service can add support a counter can't. A tracker is a tool, not a treatment.

How to pick the right goal type

Ask what you'd want to know on a random Wednesday, then use the matching shape:

  • "How many have I done?" Count-up number. The total grows, and a target and deadline show your pace.
  • "How much is left?" Count-down number. Best when the starting amount is known and the goal is zero or a lower figure.
  • "Did I do it today, or this week?" Repeated action. Choose daily only if you truly mean daily; weekly with a count is more forgiving.
  • "How long has it been?" Days without or days since. It counts up by itself, so there's nothing to log unless you reset.
  • "How long until?" A date to reach. Good for anything with a fixed day.

Two common mismatches. Tracking an outcome as a habit ("save money: done today?") gives you ticks that say nothing about the total. Tracking a routine as a number ("meditation minutes: 1,840") hides whether you showed up this week. The difference between the last two shapes is covered in streaks vs habits.

Big goals usually need two counters: the outcome and the action that moves it. A 60,000-word Aim plus a writing habit. A race countdown plus a running habit. Don't go beyond two for one goal, or the tracking becomes the project.

A starter template

Copy this into Notes and fill it in for one goal before you set up anything:

  • Vague version: what you'd say to a friend.
  • Number: what you'll count, and the target (or "none" if it's a habit or streak).
  • Date: the deadline, the event, or the review date.
  • Action: the repeated thing that moves the number, with a size and a slot ("400 words, weekday mornings").
  • Shape: count-up, count-down, habit, streak or countdown. Two at most.
  • Where you'll see it: a Home Screen widget, the Lock Screen, or a weekly check-in.

Worked through: "Read more" becomes 24 books by 31 December, 20 pages before bed, tracked as a count-up Aim with an Aim widget on the Home Screen. Five minutes of thinking, and the goal now has a home.

Questions

What are some examples of personal goals?

Read 24 books this year, save 3,000 by September, run a half marathon in April, stay alcohol-free for 90 days, or have a phone-free family dinner five nights a week. Each has a number or a date and a clear action, which is what turns a wish into a goal.

What is a good goal-setting example for students?

"Log 120 revision hours before the first final on 12 May." It has a number, a deadline and an action you can do daily. Track the hours as a count-up number and add a countdown to the exam date.

How many goals should I set at once?

Two or three active goals is plenty for most people, with no more than two counters each. More than that and the tracking starts competing with the doing.

What is the difference between a goal and a habit?

A goal has an end: a total, a zero or a date. A habit is the repeated action that gets you there. "Write 60,000 words by November" is the goal; "write 400 words on weekday mornings" is the habit.
