Focus Time Tracker for iPhone: Measure Focused Minutes, Not Hours at Your Desk

Updated January 3, 2027 · 8 min read

"I worked nine hours today" is one of the least useful sentences in productivity. Nine hours at a desk can hold two hours of real work and seven hours of email, tabs, meetings that could have been messages and that thing you looked up and then kept reading. The hours were real. The work mostly wasn't.

A focus time tracker counts something smaller and more honest: the minutes you spent on one thing on purpose. This guide covers why that number beats hours worked and Screen Time, what a realistic daily target looks like, how to start without turning it into a contest, and how to set it up on iPhone.

Why focused minutes beat hours worked

Hours worked measure presence. You can be present and stuck, present and distracted, present and doing busywork that feels like progress. Because the number goes up whatever you do, it can't tell a good day from a bad one.

Focused minutes only go up when you start a timer for one task and stay with it. That makes them a much tighter proxy for output. They're not the same as output (an hour of focus on the wrong thing is still the wrong thing), but they answer the question you actually care about at the end of a day: did I do the deep work, or did I just stay busy?

It's also something you can act on. "Work fewer hours" is vague. "One more 25-minute session before lunch" is a plan.

Why Screen Time isn't a focus tracker

Screen Time is useful, but it answers a different question. It tells you where your attention leaked: two hours in social apps, 80 pickups, 300 notifications. That's a record of what distracted you, not of what you got done.

It also can't tell writing a report from doom-scrolling, and an hour with a paper book counts as nothing. Use Screen Time to find and cut leaks; use a focus tracker to measure the work that's left once you have.

What a realistic daily focus number looks like

If you've been counting hours, you might expect six or seven focused hours a day. Almost nobody gets that, and chasing it is how people quit the tracker in week two.

From common experience, many people with desk jobs find that two to four hours of truly focused work is a good day. Some days you'll get one hour because the day is all meetings, and that's normal.

The right target is not a number from an article (including this one). It's your own baseline plus a little.

How to start: a baseline week, then a nudge

  1. Week one: just measure. Run a focus timer every time you sit down to do real work, and don't try to improve anything. You're finding out what an ordinary week looks like.
  2. Count only focused work. Writing, coding, studying, designing, planning. Email, chat and meetings don't go on the timer, even if they're important. Keep the definition strict or the number stops meaning anything.
  3. Find your baseline. Take the week's daily average, ignoring the best and worst day.
  4. Week two onward: nudge, don't leap. Aim for one more session a day than your baseline, or about 15 to 30 minutes more. If you averaged 1 hour 40, aim for 2 hours.
  5. Protect the slot, not the number. The most reliable way to raise your total is to give focus a fixed time ("first session at 9:00, before I open email") rather than hoping it fits in.

If starting is the hard part, how to focus with ADHD has practical ways to get into the first session, and they work for anyone.

How to read your focus numbers

A daily total on its own is a blunt instrument. Four numbers together tell you much more.

Daily totals

Useful for spotting patterns, not for judging single days. If Tuesdays and Thursdays are always low, look at your calendar: those are probably your meeting days, and the fix is to protect one block on them, not to try harder.

Session length

If most of your sessions end at 8 or 12 minutes, something keeps breaking them: notifications, a messy desk, a task that's too vague to start. Short sessions are a symptom worth fixing before you chase a bigger total.

Focus-to-break balance

Skipping breaks feels productive and usually isn't. A balance where breaks are roughly a fifth to a quarter of your focus time is a reasonable place to start, and the classic 25/5 rhythm sits right there.

Weekly trend

The number that matters most. Day-to-day totals swing a lot; a six-week trend shows whether your habits are actually changing. A slow climb from 9 to 12 focused hours a week is a real result, even if individual days look random.

Ways to track focus time

  • Paper tally. A mark in a notebook for every finished session. Free and visible, but weekly trends and session lengths are up to you to work out.
  • General time trackers. Apps in the style of Toggl let you start and stop a timer per project. Good for billing, less good for focus: they log any time you're "on" a project, focused or not.
  • A Pomodoro-style focus timer with history. Each session is a deliberate block of focus followed by a break, and the app keeps the log for you. This is the closest match to "focused minutes", and it's what the setup below uses. For other timer styles, see interval timer apps for iPhone.

Setting up a focus time tracker in Left

Left is an iPhone app for seeing time: how much of the day, week and year is left, countdowns, habits, and a Focus timer. Focus is part of Left Premium. Here's how to set it up as a focus tracker.

1. Set your phase lengths

Focus works in Pomodoro-style phases: Focus, Short break and Long break. The defaults are 25 minutes of focus, a 5-minute short break and a 20-minute long break after every 4 focus sessions. Each length can be anything from 1 to 60 minutes, so you can start with 15-minute sessions if 25 feels long, or move to 50 and 10 once your sessions regularly run to the end. Change them in Settings → Focus or from the clock button next to the timer.

2. Pick a timer style

Open Display options (top right) and choose one of four styles: Digital (a large countdown), Clock (an analogue dial with a wedge for the time left), Squares (one square per minute, draining as you go) or Pulse (the countdown over a soft glow in the phase color).

3. Watch the day strip

Under the timer, a strip of pills shows today's finished focus sessions and breaks at the length they actually ran, plus the one in progress. It's the quickest answer to "how much have I done today?" without opening any stats.

4. Keep the timer on your Lock Screen

While a session runs, a Live Activity shows the phase, the countdown, how many sessions you've done today and the day strip on the Lock Screen and in the Dynamic Island.

5. Add the widget

The Focus widget comes in small, medium and large sizes for the Home Screen, plus circular and rectangular sizes for the Lock Screen. On the Home Screen sizes and the rectangular Lock Screen widget there's a Start button, and a Pause or Resume button once a session exists, so you can start a session without opening the app. The Pomodoro timer page shows it in action.

Track your focus time

Reading "My focus" in Left

Tap My focus (top left in Focus) to see your history. It maps neatly onto the four numbers above:

  • Summary of your last 7 days: total focused time compared with the previous 7 days, today, daily average, average session length, number of sessions, your best day in the last 14 and your focus-to-break ratio.
  • Daily focus: a chart of the last 14 days, good for spotting your low days of the week.
  • Focus and breaks: how focus and recovery balanced this week.
  • Six-week trend: weekly focus time with your average, the number to check once a week.
  • Recent sessions and All sessions: every completed and skipped session. If you forgot to stop the timer when you got up, edit the session's start and end in All sessions, or delete it, so your totals stay honest.

A simple weekly routine: on Friday, compare your 7-day total with the week before, check the six-week trend, and pick one change for next week.

Your history is a log inside the app on your iPhone. For more on the timer itself, see the Focus timer in Left.

Pair it with an iOS Focus mode

Left's Focus timer and Apple's Focus modes are two different things with a similar name. Apple's Focus modes (Work, Personal, Do Not Disturb and so on, in the Settings app) decide which notifications and people can reach you. Left's Focus timer measures the time you spend working.

They work well together: turn on a Work Focus mode to cut notifications, then start a session in Left to measure the work. Fewer interruptions usually means longer sessions, which shows up in your average session length within a week or two. Using Left with Focus modes explains how to set it up.

Questions

What is a focus time tracker?

A focus time tracker records the minutes you spend on deliberate, single-task work, usually through timed sessions. Unlike a general time tracker, it ignores time you were merely at your desk, so the total reflects real work rather than presence.

How many hours of focused work a day is realistic?

For many people, two to four hours of truly focused work makes a good day, and some days will be less. The best target is your own baseline from a normal week, plus one extra session.

Can Screen Time track my focus time?

Not really. Screen Time shows which apps you used and how often you picked up your phone, which is useful for finding distractions, but it can't tell focused work from scrolling and doesn't count work done off the phone.

Can I start a focus session from a widget on iPhone?

Yes, with an app that has an interactive timer widget. Left's Focus widget has a Start button and a Pause or Resume button on the Home Screen and the rectangular Lock Screen size. Focus is part of Left Premium.

Is Left's Focus the same as iPhone Focus modes?

No. Apple's Focus modes filter notifications, while Left's Focus is a Pomodoro-style timer that records your focus sessions. You can use both at once: a Work Focus mode to cut interruptions and Left's timer to measure the work.
