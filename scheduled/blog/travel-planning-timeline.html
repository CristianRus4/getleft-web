Travel Planning Timeline: When to Book, Pack and Prepare

Updated November 9, 2026 · 10 min read

Big trips rarely go wrong because of packing. They go wrong because of a dull deadline nobody noticed: a passport that runs out too soon, a visa appointment with a six-week wait, the one guesthouse in town that filled up in February.

The answer is to plan backwards. Start from the day you leave, count back, and give every job the date it has to be done by. This guide does that for a big international trip, with a worked example, a compressed version for short-notice trips, a section on group trips, and a simple way to keep it all in front of you on your iPhone.

Sort every job into one of three kinds

  • Blockers can stop you boarding or entering the country: passport validity, visas and entry permits, and in some places proof of certain vaccinations. They often sit in someone else's queue, so you can't rush them at the end.
  • Sell-outs get worse or disappear as time passes: flights on popular dates, accommodation in small or busy places, timed-entry tickets, trains that need a reservation.
  • Last-minute jobs can't be done early, or are pointless to do early: online check-in, packing, emptying the fridge.

The rule: blockers first, whatever your lead time. Sell-outs next. Last-minute jobs stay last. Every stage below is that rule applied to the calendar.

6+ months out: documents and big decisions

  • Fix the dates and the budget. Everything else hangs off the departure date, so agree it first, along with a rough total and the time off from work or school.
  • Check your passport today. Compare its expiry with your return date, not your departure date. Some countries want several months of validity beyond your stay, and some want blank pages. If you need a renewal, apply now: it can take weeks, and longer before the summer rush.
  • Check the entry rules. Visas, electronic travel authorizations and entry permits depend on your nationality and your destination, and they change. Use official sources: your government's travel advice (in the UK, for example, GOV.UK foreign travel advice) and the destination's official embassy or immigration website. Be wary of look-alike sites that charge a fee to file the form for you.
  • Book any visa appointment. If your visa needs an in-person appointment, the wait for a slot can be longer than the processing itself.

3 to 6 months out: book what sells out

  • Flights. There's no magic booking day. For popular dates, like school holidays, festivals and the end of the year, booking earlier usually gives you more choice of times and fares. Decide the price you'd be happy with, set a fare alert, and book when it comes close. Read the change rules before you pay.
  • The accommodation that sells out. You don't need every night booked now. Book the ones that are hard to replace: a small town with few places to stay, a national park lodge, a festival week, the first night after a long-haul flight. Choose free cancellation where you can and leave the easy cities for later.
  • Transport with reservations. Long-distance trains, ferries and peak-season car hire can fill up. Book them alongside the accommodation they connect.
  • Travel insurance. Buy it soon after paying for the big non-refundable things. With many policies, cancellation cover starts on the day you buy, so a policy bought the week before you fly won't help if something goes wrong in March.

1 to 2 months out: health, tickets and the plan

  • See a travel clinic if your destination needs it. Some vaccines need more than one dose or time to work, so don't leave this to the last two weeks. A travel clinic or your doctor can tell you what applies to your route and your health, and whether your regular medicines have entry rules. The CDC's Travelers' Health pages are useful reading before the appointment.
  • Book timed-entry attractions. Popular museums, tours, permits and well-known restaurants often release tickets on a set date. Find out when, and be ready on that day.
  • Sketch a rough itinerary. Just where you sleep each night and how you get between places, including how you get from the airport to the first bed. If you'll drive, check whether that country asks for an international driving permit.
  • Note the final payment dates. Tours and group bookings often have one. Put it somewhere you'll actually see it.

2 weeks out: money, phone and documents

  • Money. Check what your cards charge abroad. Take two cards, kept in different places, plus a little local cash for arrival.
  • Phone. Choose between roaming and a travel eSIM. For an eSIM, check your iPhone supports it and is unlocked, buy it now, and follow the provider's instructions on when to turn it on.
  • Copies of everything. Save your passport, visa, insurance and bookings somewhere you can open offline, and share them and your itinerary with someone at home.
  • Confirm bookings. Reread every confirmation for dates, names and arrival times. A hotel expecting you on the wrong night is easy to fix now and hard to fix at midnight.

Final week: packing, check-in and home

  • Write the packing list early in the week, pack two days before. The list shows what you still need to buy while there's time. Check your baggage allowance and liquid rules.
  • Know when check-in opens. For many airlines it's a day or two before the flight, but it varies. Check in as soon as it opens if you care where you sit.
  • Load your phone. Boarding passes in Wallet, offline maps of your first stops, and something to watch on the plane.
  • Prepare the house. Pets, plants and post sorted, fridge run down, bins out, out-of-office on.

The day before and the day of

Day before: passport, cards, phone, chargers and keys go in one place. Charge everything. Check your flight status and plan the journey to the airport with the buffer your airline recommends, then add a little more. Day of: check for the passport one last time before you lock the door, then stop planning. Almost anything you forgot can be bought or fixed when you land.

The short-notice version

Booked something three weeks out? The same three kinds of jobs apply, squeezed into days:

  1. Today: blockers. Check passport validity and entry rules before you pay for anything. If a visa can't be done in time, changing destination now is far cheaper than losing a flight.
  2. Within two days: sell-outs. Flights, the first nights of accommodation and anything with limited places. Buy insurance the same day.
  3. First week: health and tickets. If your destination calls for vaccines, call a travel clinic straight away and ask what's still possible in the time you have.
  4. Final week: the normal final week. Money, phone, copies, packing, check-in, house. These take the same time whether you planned for six months or six days.

Planning a group trip

Group trips fail in their own way: everyone assumes someone else booked it. The timeline stays the same; what changes is who owns each job.

  • Agree one shared date first. Nothing gets booked until the dates are fixed. Run a quick poll, pick the dates most people can do, and call them final. Then put the trip on everyone's phone as a Joint Ahead date in Left (part of Left Premium), so the whole group counts down to the same departure.
  • Give every booking one owner. One person books the accommodation, one the car, one the timed tickets. If people fly from different cities, each books their own flights and shares the confirmation in one shared note.
  • Set a pay-back date. Whoever pays for shared bookings gets the money back by a set date, before the final balance is due. Make that a countdown too.
  • Agree the budget level up front. Hostel or hotel, street food or restaurants. Settling it early saves an argument at every later step.
  • Find one call time. If the group is spread across countries, the time zone meeting planner finds an hour that suits everyone.

Questions

How far in advance should I book flights for an international trip?

There's no single right number. For popular dates such as school holidays and festivals, booking several months ahead usually gives you more choice, so set a fare alert early and book when the price is one you'd accept.

When should I book hotels for a big trip?

Book the places that are hard to replace first, like small towns, national parks and festival weeks, three to six months out or earlier. Cities with plenty of choice can usually wait.

How early should I check my passport before traveling?

As soon as the trip is an idea, and at least six months before you leave. Compare the expiry with your return date, since some countries require validity for months beyond your stay.

When should I buy travel insurance?

Soon after you pay for the big non-refundable parts of the trip. With many policies, cancellation cover starts only when you buy, so buying early protects you for longer.

What should I do the week before a trip?

Write the packing list, check in online when it opens, load boarding passes and offline maps onto your phone, and get the house ready. Pack a day or two before, not the night before.
