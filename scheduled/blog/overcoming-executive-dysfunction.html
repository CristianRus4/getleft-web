How to Overcome Executive Dysfunction, One Stuck Moment at a Time

Updated November 9, 2026 · 10 min read

Executive dysfunction rarely feels like a diagnosis while it's happening. It feels like standing in the kitchen, knowing the dishes need doing, and not moving. Or a pile of post you've stepped around for three weeks. You know what to do. The part of your brain that turns knowing into doing has stalled.

General advice like "break it down" is true, but it doesn't tell you what to do in the next sixty seconds. This guide is organized by the specific moment you get stuck. Name the moment, use a move for that moment, and then, when you have a calm hour, set up the small things that make the moment come up less often.

Executive dysfunction shows up with ADHD, autism, depression, anxiety, burnout and brain injury, and on a bad week it shows up in almost anyone. Nothing here is a treatment. It's a practical kit for the stuck moments themselves.

The six stuck moments at a glance

Most stuck moments fall into one of six patterns. Each needs a different move, which is why one strategy never covers all of them.

1. You can't start

What's happening: the task is stored in your head as one big, vague block ("sort out the taxes"), and a vague block has no handle to grab. Starting takes a burst of effort, and a vague task demands the biggest burst.

  • Make the first step physical and tiny. Not "do the taxes" but "open the laptop and put the folder next to it". A good first step is something your hands do, and it's so small it feels slightly silly.
  • Set a 5-minute timer and promise only five minutes. You're allowed to stop when it rings. Often you won't, because the hard part was the first thirty seconds.
  • Move your body before your brain. Stand up and walk to where the task lives. Being in the room with the dishes is half of doing the dishes.

Starting is the most common stuck moment, so it has its own guide: our task initiation strategies go much deeper into floors, implementation intentions and body doubling.

2. You can't switch

What's happening: switching means letting go of one thing and loading another, and both halves cost effort. If you're absorbed, the clock disappears. If you're not absorbed, you're simply parked, and a parked brain resists moving.

  • Use a warning alarm, not just a deadline alarm. One alarm ten minutes before you need to switch ("wrap up"), one at the switch ("go now"). The first gives your brain time to unhook.
  • Leave a parking note. Before you stop, write one line: where you are and what the very next step is. Leaving feels much easier when you know you won't lose your place.
  • Add a bridge. A two-minute ritual between tasks, like refilling your water or stepping outside, marks the end of one thing and the start of the next.

3. You can't stop scrolling

What's happening: scrolling asks nothing of you, and stopping asks you to decide what comes next. Often the scroll is a hiding place from a task you can't start, or a sign that you're simply out of fuel.

  • Name the next physical action before you put the phone down. "Put the kettle on." Without a next action, putting the phone down just leaves a gap, and the phone fills it again.
  • Stand up. Changing posture breaks the loop more reliably than willpower does.
  • Put the phone somewhere you have to walk to. Charging it across the room adds just enough friction.
  • Check the clock honestly. If it's 11pm, the right move isn't productivity, it's bed. Scrolling at night is often tiredness that hasn't been noticed yet.

If the phone is the main thing pulling you away during work, our guide on how to focus with ADHD covers turning it into a focus partner instead.

4. You can't decide

What's happening: when several options look equally important, your brain keeps weighing them and never lands. Each decision costs energy, so a day full of small choices drains you before the real work begins.

  • Choose for yourself in advance with a default rule. "When two tasks tie, do the one with the nearest deadline." "If I can't pick lunch in a minute, it's the usual." Decide the rule once, on a calm day, and let it decide for you on a stuck day.
  • Cut the options to two. Cross out everything except two choices, then pick.
  • Give yourself sixty seconds, then the default wins. If the timer runs out, you do whatever your rule says. Most decisions are reversible, and a fine choice made now beats a perfect one never made.

5. You forgot the thing

What's happening: working memory, the mental notepad that holds "take the parcel when you leave", is small and easily wiped. If something isn't in front of you, it effectively doesn't exist.

  • Capture it the second you think of it. Ask Siri to remind you, or add it to Apple Reminders. Not in a minute: now.
  • Attach it to a place or a time, not to your memory. Apple Reminders can remind you when you arrive at or leave a place ("remind me to return the parcel when I leave home") or at a set time. A location reminder fires at the moment you can actually act on it.
  • Put the cue where your eyes already go. Your Lock Screen is the most looked-at surface you own. Our visual reminders setup for iPhone walks through using it.

6. You're overwhelmed by a pile

What's happening: a pile of laundry, emails or tasks reads as one huge threat rather than many small jobs. Your brain can't see the parts, so it can't pick one.

  • Shrink the pile to one visible item. Take one thing off the pile and hide the rest: close the other tabs, put the papers in a box, turn the list face down. Deal with the one thing in front of you, then take the next.
  • Sort by one rule only. "Everything that's rubbish first." "Only emails from people I know." One rule turns a jumble into a straight line.
  • Work for ten minutes and allow yourself to stop. A pile you chip at for ten minutes a day disappears faster than one you wait to tackle all at once.
  • Count what's done, not what's left. "I cleared twelve emails" keeps you going. "There are 300 left" stops you.

Energy and rest are part of the system

Executive function runs on fuel. The same task that takes ten minutes after a good night's sleep can feel impossible after a bad one. Hunger, stress and illness drain the same tank.

  • Put hard tasks in your best hours. If your head is clearest at 10am, protect that slot for the thing you'd otherwise avoid. Use the slumps for admin and tidying.
  • Have a low-energy version of each important task. Full version: clean the kitchen. Low-energy version: clear the counter. On a bad day the low-energy version counts.
  • Treat rest as maintenance, not a reward. If you only rest once everything is done, you'll never rest, and the stuck moments multiply.

Self-compassion is a practical tool

This isn't a feel-good extra. Shame has a direct cost: every time you call yourself lazy for being stuck, the task picks up an extra layer of dread, and the next start gets harder. Being kind to yourself makes the next start cheaper.

  • Use a one-line script. "Stuck, not lazy. What's the smallest next move?" It names the moment and points straight at the fix.
  • Restart, don't repair. After a missed day, the next day is just the next day. Don't try to make up the lost time; it turns one task into two.
  • Notice where you get stuck, like a scientist. If you always freeze at the same point, that's not a flaw in you, it's useful information. It tells you which setup to add.

When to get more help

If stuck moments are getting in the way of work, relationships, money or looking after yourself, it's worth talking to someone. A clinician can look at what's behind it, whether that's ADHD, depression, burnout or something else, and an occupational therapist can help you build strategies around your actual day. Apps, alarms and timers are tools that support you; they don't replace that help.

Questions

What is executive dysfunction?

Executive dysfunction is difficulty with the mental skills that plan, start, switch, stop and remember tasks. It isn't a diagnosis on its own; it shows up with ADHD, autism, depression, burnout, brain injury and other conditions.

How do you overcome executive dysfunction in the moment?

Name which stuck moment you're in, then use a move for that moment. For starting, make the first step physical and tiny and set a 5-minute timer; for a pile, shrink it to one visible item.

Is executive dysfunction the same as being lazy?

No. Laziness is not wanting to do something. Executive dysfunction is wanting to and not being able to get going, and treating it as laziness usually makes it worse.

What are some quick executive dysfunction tips?

Set a warning alarm ten minutes before you need to switch, capture reminders the second you think of them, use a default rule for decisions, and stand up when you can't stop scrolling.

Can an iPhone app help with executive dysfunction?

An app can't fix the cause, but it can hold the cues and timers so your brain doesn't have to. Widgets for habits and countdowns, a one-tap focus timer and location-based reminders all reduce how much you need to remember.
