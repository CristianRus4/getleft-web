Job Application Tracker: How to Track a Job Search on iPhone

Updated November 9, 2026 · 8 min read

Most job application trackers answer one question: where does each application stand? That matters. Without it, you forget who you emailed, miss a follow-up, and apply to the same role twice.

But a job search has a second problem that a list of companies doesn't solve. It's long, and most of it is silence. You send ten applications and hear nothing for two weeks. The list looks the same every day, so it starts to feel like nothing is happening, and that's when people stop applying. So this guide splits the tracker in two: the pipeline, which records every application, and the pace, which tracks the work you control and keeps you going while you wait.

Part 1: the pipeline

The pipeline is a record of every role you've touched. Keep it boring and complete. Its job is to make sure nothing falls through the cracks.

What to record for each application

  • Company and role, as written in the posting.
  • Link to the posting. Save a copy of the text too: postings often disappear once the role closes, right before your interview.
  • Date applied. This tells you when to follow up and when to stop waiting.
  • Status, from the stages below.
  • Contact: the recruiter, hiring manager or the person who referred you, with how you reached them.
  • Next step and its date: "Follow up with Dana, Thursday" or "Second interview, 14 Nov, 10:00".

The last column is the one that does the work. A status tells you where an application is. A next step with a date tells you what to do about it. If a row has no next step, either add one or close it.

Status stages

Six stages are enough. More than that and you'll spend time deciding which one fits.

StageMeansTypical next step
SavedYou found it and want to applyApply by a date, or drop it
AppliedApplication sentFollow up after about a week
ScreeningA recruiter or first callSend thanks, confirm the next round
InterviewOne or more interview roundsPrepare, then follow up after each round
OfferYou have an offerCompare, negotiate, decide by a date
ClosedRejected, withdrawn or no replyNone. Note what you learned

Move an application to Closed when you've had no reply for a few weeks after a follow-up. Leaving it in Applied keeps a false sense of how many chances are still open.

Where to keep it

  • A spreadsheet. One row per application, one column per field above. Sort by next-step date each morning. It's free, works on any device, and you can't over-design it.
  • A board. A Notion or Trello-style board with one column per stage and one card per application. Dragging a card from Applied to Interview feels good, and the board shows where your applications pile up.
  • A dedicated job tracker. Some apps and browser extensions save postings straight from job sites and add reminders. Useful if you apply a lot; overkill if you apply to five carefully chosen roles a month.

Pick one and stop there. The best pipeline is the one you update the same day you apply.

Part 2: the pace

Here's the uncomfortable part. You don't control whether a company replies, whether the role gets frozen, or whether someone internal gets it. If you judge each week by outcomes, most weeks will look like failure even when you did everything right.

So track inputs instead. These are the things you decide:

  • Applications sent per week, each one tailored.
  • Networking messages: a note to a former colleague, a question to someone at a company you like, a coffee request.
  • Follow-ups sent on time.
  • Prep sessions for interviews, portfolio work or a skill you're brushing up.

If you sent your five applications and two messages this week, it was a good week, whatever your inbox says. That's not a trick to feel better. Inputs are what produce outcomes over a long search, and they're the only lever you have.

Set a weekly target

Choose a number you can hit in a bad week, not a heroic one. For many people that's something like 5 applications and 2 networking messages a week, but it depends on your field and how much each application takes. Ten rushed applications usually do worse than four good ones. If you hit the target easily for three weeks in a row, raise it a little.

Then turn the weekly target into a total with a date. Five a week for eight weeks is 40 applications by early January. A total gives you something that fills up over time, which a silent inbox never does.

Schedule follow-ups

Every application gets a follow-up date the moment you send it: about a week later is a common rhythm. Put it in the pipeline's next-step column, and check that column first thing each day. A short, polite follow-up is normal and sometimes the reason an application gets read at all.

Protect your energy

  • A daily cap. Decide on a maximum, such as three applications a day. Past that, quality drops and you burn out by week three.
  • A stop time. End the search at a set hour, say 4 pm, and don't check job sites or email after it. Searching all day isn't more effective; it's just more tiring.
  • A "days since I started" view. Counting the days of the search sounds grim, but it reframes the silence. "Day 46" makes it clear you're in the middle of a long process, not failing at a short one. Next to your input totals, it shows how much you've done in that time.
  • Start small on hard days. If opening the laptop feels impossible, commit to one task: rewrite one bullet on your CV, or send one message. Our guide on getting started on tasks has more ways to break the freeze, and they work for anyone.

A simple weekly routine

  1. Monday: sort the pipeline by next-step date. Send every follow-up that's due.
  2. During the week: apply in focused sessions, log each one on the Aim, tap the weekly habits.
  3. After every interview: send thanks the same day, update the status and next step.
  4. Friday: check the Aim's pace and the weekly habits. Close stale applications. Then stop for the weekend.

The pipeline tells you what to do next. The pace tells you that you're doing enough. You need both to get through a long search without losing track or losing heart.

Questions

What should a job application tracker include?

At minimum: company, role, link to the posting, date applied, status, contact, and the next step with its date. The next step and date matter most, because they tell you what to do and when.

What are the stages of a job application?

A simple set is saved, applied, screening, interview, offer, and closed (rejected, withdrawn or no reply). More stages than that usually make the tracker harder to keep up to date.

How many job applications should I send per week?

Choose a number you can sustain in a bad week, often something like 3 to 5 tailored applications, plus a couple of networking messages. Quality usually beats volume, so raise the target only once it feels easy.
