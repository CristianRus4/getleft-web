Weight Loss Timeline: How Long It Really Takes, and How to Track It Without Obsessing

Updated November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

Most weight loss plans fail on the timeline, not the method. People expect the first week's drop to continue every week, set a date that was never realistic, and quit when the scale stalls in week five. A realistic weight loss timeline sets the right expectations up front, so a slow week reads as normal instead of as failure.

This guide covers what's typical at each stage, how to work out a sensible target date, and how to track progress on your iPhone in a way that keeps you going. It's general information, not medical advice. Talk to your doctor before starting, especially if you have a health condition, take medication, or plan to lose a lot of weight.

What's a realistic rate?

Health bodies such as the CDC and the NHS generally describe a gradual, steady loss of about 0.5 to 1 kg (1 to 2 lb) per week as a sustainable pace for most adults. Faster loss is possible early on and in some medical programs, but a slower pace is usually easier to keep up and to keep off.

The weight loss timeline, stage by stage

Week 1: the fast start

The first week often shows the biggest drop on the scale. Much of it is water and stored carbohydrate, not fat. It's encouraging, but it isn't the pace to expect from here on.

Weeks 2 to 4: the honest pace

Loss settles into the steady rate. Some weeks show nothing, then a drop arrives at once. Daily weigh-ins swing up and down with water, salt, sleep and hormones. This is when the trend matters more than any single number.

Months 2 to 3: visible changes

Clothes fit differently, and other people may notice. Habits that felt like effort in week one start to feel normal. This is often where energy, sleep and fitness improve too, which can be better motivation than the scale.

Months 3 to 6: the plateau

Almost everyone hits a stretch of weeks where weight barely moves. As you get lighter, your body needs less energy, so the same habits produce a smaller loss. It's normal and expected, not a sign the plan has stopped working. A doctor or dietitian can help you adjust.

After you reach your goal: maintenance

Maintenance is a phase of its own, and it deserves a plan. Many people find the habits that got them there need to stay, at a slightly looser level, for the result to last.

How to set a target date

  1. Pick a goal weight with your doctor, or a first milestone such as 5% of your starting weight, which is meaningful for health in many people and much less daunting.
  2. Divide by a modest weekly rate. Use the lower end, 0.5 kg or 1 lb a week, unless your doctor suggests otherwise.
  3. Add 20% for real life. A 20-week plan becomes 24 weeks.
  4. Put the date on a calendar. Our add or subtract date calculator gives you the date that's 24 weeks from today.

Example: starting at 90 kg with a first goal of 80 kg. 10 kg at 0.5 kg a week is 20 weeks, plus 20% is 24 weeks. Starting on January 5, that's a target around June 21. A goal that lands in summer, with a few weeks to spare, is one you can actually believe.

Track the trend, not the day

A single weigh-in tells you very little. A few habits make tracking calmer and more useful:

  • Weigh at the same time, ideally first thing in the morning, in similar clothes.
  • Look at the weekly average or the line over a month, not today versus yesterday.
  • Track one or two non-scale measures: a waist measurement every two weeks, how a pair of jeans fits, how far you can walk.
  • Track the habits you control. You can't make the scale move today. You can walk, cook at home, or go to bed on time. Those are worth counting.

Questions

How long does it take to lose 10 kg (22 lb)?

At a steady 0.5 to 1 kg (1 to 2 lb) a week, about 10 to 20 weeks, often a little longer once plateaus and holidays are included. Talk to your doctor about a pace that suits you.

Why do I lose so much weight in the first week?

Much of the early drop is water and stored carbohydrate rather than fat. After the first week or two, loss usually settles into a slower, steadier pace.

Why has my weight loss stopped?

Plateaus are common, especially after a few months, partly because a lighter body needs less energy. Look at the trend over several weeks, and talk to a doctor or dietitian if it lasts.

How often should I weigh myself?

Whatever keeps you calm and consistent. Some people weigh daily and watch the weekly average; others weigh once a week at the same time. The trend is what matters.
