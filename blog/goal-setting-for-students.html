Goal Setting for Students: A Term-by-Term Method That Works

Updated November 12, 2026 · 9 min read

Most goal setting advice is written for adults with an open calendar. Students don't have one. Your year is already cut into terms or semesters, with exam weeks, coursework deadlines and holidays fixed months in advance by someone else. A goal that ignores that shape ("do better at school this year") has nowhere to live.

So set goals by the term, not the year. A term is long enough to change a grade and short enough that the end date feels real. This guide gives you a five-step method built around that calendar, worked examples for high school, university and exam years, and a short iPhone setup at the end.

Why the year is the wrong unit for student goals

A year-long goal set in September gives you nine months of "plenty of time" followed by six weeks of panic. Nothing in the goal tells you whether October was a good month. The term fixes three things at once:

  • A real deadline. The last day of term and the exam timetable are already decided. You don't have to invent urgency.
  • A natural review point. Every holiday is a chance to look back and set the next term's goals with actual evidence.
  • A smaller failure. If one term goes badly, you lose 12 weeks, not a year, and the next term starts clean.

The year still matters for direction ("get into the course I want"). The term is where you turn that direction into something you can do this week.

The term-by-term method

Five steps. The first three take about 30 minutes in the first week of term. The last two are habits you keep.

1. One goal per area, three areas at most

Pick up to three areas and write one goal for each:

  • Grades or learning: the subject or skill that needs the most work this term.
  • Health or life: sleep, exercise, a part-time job, money, getting to class on time.
  • Something personal: an instrument, a sport, a language, a side project, reading for fun.

Three is a ceiling, not a target. One goal you actually work on beats five you mean to. If you're in an exam term, it's fine to run only the first area and keep the other two very small.

2. Turn each goal into a weekly action and a number

A goal you can't do this week is a wish. For each goal, write the action you will repeat every week and the number that shows you did it:

The number is what you track. Work backwards from the deadline to check it's possible: 12 papers in 11 weeks means roughly one a week with one spare. If the maths doesn't work, change the goal now, not in week 9. Our SMART goals guide has more rewrites if a goal still feels vague.

3. Put the fixed dates where you can see them

Write down the dates that shape the term: the first exam, coursework deadlines, mock exams, the last day of term. Then put the one or two that matter most somewhere you look every day, like your Lock Screen. "34 days until the maths exam" changes how a free evening feels in a way a date in a PDF timetable never does. To see how far through the term you are as a percentage, try the semester countdown calculator.

4. A 10-minute Sunday check

Once a week, answer three questions and nothing more:

  1. Did I hit each weekly number? Yes, no or partly.
  2. If not, what got in the way: time, energy, or not knowing where to start?
  3. What exact slot this week will the action go in?

That's it. No new goals, no rewriting the plan. If you also plan your whole week on Sunday, the time management tips for students cover that bigger routine; this check is just for the goals.

5. An end-of-term review that feeds the next term

In the first days of the holiday, before you forget how the term felt, spend 20 minutes on four questions:

  • Which goals did I hit, and which weekly numbers did I actually keep?
  • Which week did things slip, and why?
  • What did my results or teacher feedback say about where marks are being lost?
  • What carries over? Keep, shrink, or drop each goal.

The answers become next term's goals. If you averaged 5 study hours a week against a target of 8, next term's target is 6, not 10. Goals built on last term's evidence are the ones that hold.

Process goals vs grade goals

A grade is a result. It depends on how you work, but also on the paper, the marking, the grade boundaries and how you slept the night before. You don't fully control it, so it makes a poor thing to track week to week.

A process goal is the work you do control: papers completed, hours studied, questions redone. Keep the grade goal as the direction and track the process goal:

  • Grade goal: raise maths from C to B by June.
  • Process goal: two marked past-paper sections a week, every wrong answer redone within two days.

If you hit the process goal and the grade doesn't move, that's useful information. Show your marked papers to your teacher and ask what's costing you marks. The method changes; the effort was not wasted.

Examples by stage

High school

  • Learning: raise maths from C to B by June. Weekly: two past-paper sections on Tuesday and Thursday. Number: sections done.
  • Life: phone out of the bedroom on school nights. Weekly: five nights. Number: nights done.
  • Personal: learn three full songs on guitar this term. Weekly: three 20-minute practice sessions.

University

  • Learning: 10 hours of independent study a week this semester. Weekly: fixed blocks between lectures. Number: hours logged against a semester target of 120.
  • Life: run 3 times a week this term. Number: runs per week.
  • Personal: save for the summer trip. Weekly: a fixed amount from each paycheck. Number: money saved.

Exam year

  • Learning: complete 12 past papers before 1 May, marked against the mark scheme. Weekly: one full paper on Saturday morning under timed conditions.
  • Life: in bed by 11 on school nights during the revision term. Number: nights.
  • Personal: keep one thing that isn't study, like a weekly match or a club, and protect it. In an exam year, this goal is about not burning out, so keep it small and don't drop it.

A note for teachers and parents

Students stick with goals they chose. Help with the method (one goal per area, a weekly number, a Sunday check) and let them pick the goals. Ask about the process numbers rather than the grade: "How many papers this week?" is an easier conversation than "Why is it still a C?" For students who find planning especially hard, our guide to study tools for students with ADHD has setups that need less willpower.

When the term goes off track

Illness, a family problem or a heavy coursework week will knock out some weeks. When it happens, don't try to catch up all the missed work at once. Recalculate: how many weeks are left, and what's the new realistic number? Cutting 12 papers to 9 and hitting it beats sticking to 12 and quietly giving up in March. The end-of-term review will tell you what to change next time.

Questions

What are good goals for students?

Good student goals have a deadline tied to the school calendar and a weekly action you control, like "complete 12 past papers before 1 May" or "study 10 hours a week this semester". Keep to one goal each for learning, health and something personal.

How many goals should a student set?

Three at most per term, one in each area. In an exam term, one main study goal plus two very small ones is usually enough.

Should students set goals for grades?

Use a grade as the direction, but track a process goal you control, such as papers completed or hours studied. Grades also depend on the paper, the marking and the day, so they make a poor weekly measure.

Why set goals by term instead of by year?

The term has fixed exam dates and a clear end, so the deadline feels real and you get a natural review point every holiday. A bad term costs you weeks, not a whole year.

How can I track study goals on my iPhone?

Use a countdown for your exam date, a counter goal for study hours or past papers, and a weekly habit for the action. In Left these are Ahead countdowns, Aims and habits, and each can sit on your Home Screen or Lock Screen as a widget.
